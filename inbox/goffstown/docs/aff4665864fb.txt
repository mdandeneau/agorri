Informational Session with Goffstown Fire Department

Chair Dubrulle introduced the session, noting that the informational meeting format had been
used previously with DPW Director Adam Jacobs for a discussion on the road plan, and
encouraged members to revisit that session on their own time to avoid re-covering that
material. She then turned the floor over to Fire Chief Selleck.

Retention and the Staffing Crisis

Fire Chief Selleck opened by providing historical context for the Goffstown Fire Department.
He explained that when the department was largely volunteer based, drawing from three
merged departments, the town had a roster of approximately 80 call personnel. Today, only
two call personnel have made more than three calls year-to-date, both hired prior to 1991.
Since 1991, the department has hired 23 call members, of whom only five remain on the
roster. Chief Selleck was clear that this is not a Goffstown-specific problem, but a national
decline in volunteerism driven by changing family and economic dynamics.

The department transitioned to full-time staffing on weekdays beginning in 1987, added part-
time and per diem coverage for nights, and moved entirely to 24-hour shifts in 2020 due to
the difficulty of retaining part-time and per diem personnel.

Chief Selleck identified retention as the single largest challenge and the root cause
underlying its cost and operational difficulties. Since 2020, the department has lost 31 full-
time personnel against a total of 24 full-time positions, with a median service length of 1.1
years. Of those who departed, 24 left for larger departments, three left the fire service

Direct Costs of Turnover

Chief Selleck presented a breakdown of the direct costs associated with each new hire,
including tuition for required certifications, PFAS-free protective gear (noting that all
operational personnel are on track to receive one set by year's end), uniforms, physicals,
criminal background checks, mandatory overtime, and wages and benefits, which vary
significantly depending on age and family status. He noted that most losses occur within the
first three years of employment, which often coincides with when employees are starting
families, driving benefit costs higher just before they depart.

He noted that training costs are compounded by federal law, which requires the department to
compensate personnel for time spent in mandatory classes. When a firefighter is sent to
training, their shift position either goes vacant or must be backfilled with overtime.

Overtime Analysis

Chief Selleck walked the committee through the department's overtime data, emphasizing
that some level of overtime is unavoidable, driven by vacation, bereavement, injury, and
mandatory department physicals. However, he stressed that the education-related overtime
component is dramatically inflated compared to peer departments, precisely because
Goffstown hires new entrants who require extensive training from the ground up. He
estimated that education-related overtime should be approximately one-fifth of what it
currently is, if the department were able to consistently hire fully certified personnel.

He also clarified that overtime and leave hours will not arithmetically reconcile in budget

Budget Impact and the Default Budget

Chair Dubrulle asked Chief Selleck to explain the impact of the default budget on his
department. Chief Selleck explained that the overtime line has historically been under-
budgeted, with actual expenditures consistently exceeding appropriations. He provided
specific figures:

      FY2024: Budgeted $341,935 in overtime; actual expenditure was $377,307.
      FY2025: Budgeted $348,811; actual expenditure was $454,515.
      FY2026: Following the reversion to the default budget, the overtime appropriation
       dropped back to approximately the FY2024 level, creating a significant funding gap.

Because wages and benefits represent approximately $3.5 million, or roughly 80% of the
department's total budget, there is very little capacity in the remaining operational lines to
absorb overtime overruns. Chief Selleck described having to zero out lines such as fire
prevention materials and defer preventative maintenance on apparatus simply to preserve
staffing levels. He noted that in FY2026, this constraint limited the department to hiring only
four personnel rather than the six needed to maintain full shift coverage.

Jessica Peutin asked why the overtime line had not been increased in prior budgets given the
historical overspend. The Finance Director confirmed that while requests had been made,
reductions were applied by the appropriations committee, and that when budgets did not pass,
proposed adjustments could not take effect.

Engine Company Size and Operational Impact

Chief Selleck distributed a handout on NFPA 1710 engine company standards and explained
the operational significance of crew size. The national standard calls for four-person engine
companies operating on a two-in, two-out protocol: two personnel engaged in fire attack or
search and rescue, and two personnel outside managing water supply, ground ladders, and
serving as a rapid intervention team. The standard also requires 17 firefighters on scene.
Chief Selleck noted that Goffstown relies on mutual aid from Manchester and Bedford to
approach this number, but that surrounding volunteer departments are increasingly unable to
respond or arrive with only a single unstaffed operator.

He presented data from a NIST study showing that smaller crew sizes result in longer time-
to-water, greater spread of fire, and significantly elevated cardiovascular stress on
firefighters. Statistically, firefighter injuries and fatalities are most likely to occur in the first
10 minutes of a structural fire. He stated: "We die of heart attacks… our heart rates are
operating for so long at the maximum."

Chief Selleck illustrated the real-world consequences by recounting the department's
response to a nighttime structure fire on Sunny Street. The first-arriving crew, operating
below standard strength, had to sequentially perform a ladder rescue of one trapped victim,
make entry to search for a second victim, perform CPR, and re-enter to extinguish the fire
after the attack hose was burned through. He noted that in a fully staffed department, each of
those tasks would have been performed simultaneously by separate four-person companies.

He further illustrated experience gaps using fire helmets labeled with years of service
representing each of the department's four shifts. He demonstrated how quickly on-duty
experience drops when a single officer or senior firefighter is absent, leaving stations with as
little as two combined years of fire experience, or no certified officer, to handle emergency
calls independently.

Per Diem Staffing and Recruitment Efforts

In response to a question, Chief Selleck explained that the department no longer employs per
diem firefighters due to a poor return on investment. Per diem EMS personnel are used, but
managing a 24-hour position through per diem staffing requires a pool of 30 to 40
individuals, and engagement tends to be inconsistent. The department is participating in a
regional EMS study grant examining alternative staffing models.

Chief Selleck highlighted the Department of Defense SkillBridge internship program as the
department's most effective recruitment tool, noting it had generated 30 applicants per
position and produced several current full-time hires. He expressed concern that no new
interns have been approved through the program since September of the prior year, which he
attributed to current military operational demands.

Jessica Peutin asked about high school and trade school outreach. Chief Selleck confirmed
the department is exploring participation in the Mike Rowe Works Foundation program and
continues to attend job fairs and partner with a local college for student volunteers. He noted
that the Department of Labor no longer permits under-18 interns to ride on apparatus when
responding to emergency calls, which limits the effectiveness of high school programs.

Regionalization

Chief Selleck noted that he has convened discussions with representatives from three
neighboring communities about regionalization of fire and EMS services, an uncommon
development in New Hampshire, where town-by-town control is deeply ingrained. He
acknowledged that the primary obstacle to regionalization is governance and oversight:
aligning multiple elected boards on a shared operational model is extremely difficult. He
stated that the department is "beyond the fat… beyond the muscle, and we're hitting bone
now," and that all communities involved must consider models they would not otherwise
entertain.

Jack Carbonneau asked about mutual aid billing practices. Chief Selleck explained that
mutual aid is not formally billed between communities. He noted that Goffstown frequently
responds to Manchester on ambulance mutual aid calls, and that Manchester reciprocates
with fully staffed engine and ladder companies at structure fires, a ratio he finds roughly
balanced. He has recently implemented a policy to limit the frequency of ambulance mutual
aid responses to Manchester, consistent with practices in neighboring communities.

EMS Revenue

Jessica Peutin asked whether the town's ambulance service constitutes a revenue stream.
Chair Dubrulle and Chief Selleck confirmed that ambulance billing generates approximately
$1 million per year, held in a revolving fund that covers EMS equipment, ambulance
replacements (currently approximately $300,000 each), and a portion of personnel costs,
without drawing on property tax revenue. Chief Selleck noted that the department receives
approximately 30 cents on the dollar for a Medicare transport, which represents the largest
payer category given the town's aging demographics, and that costs are partially shifted to
private insurers to compensate.

Proposed Solution: Shift Captain Positions

Chief Selleck presented his proposal to address the retention crisis through the creation of
four Shift Captain positions, one per shift. He framed the proposal as a single intervention
designed to simultaneously address the top three reasons cited in exit interviews: workload,
lack of supervision, and lack of upward mobility.

Adding a Shift Captain at the downtown station would bring that station to four personnel per
shift, always provide a qualified supervisor at each station, and create a meaningful
promotion pathway for current firefighters. The positions would be non-union, outside the
existing bargaining unit that covers lieutenants and firefighters.

Chief Selleck emphasized his intention to promote from within rather than hire externally,
preserving institutional knowledge, including familiarity with local geography, apparatus, and
station layouts, and minimizing the morale impact of outside hires. He noted that promoting
internal candidates would create a cascade effect, with lieutenants moving up and new
firefighter positions becoming available at the entry level, where the department now has an
eligibility list for the first time in its history.

He acknowledged that this proposal would not produce immediate cost savings, and that the
transition period, during which newly promoted officers are still building competency and
new entry-level firefighters are being trained, will involve some overlap of costs. However,
he expressed confidence that if annual attrition could be reduced from the current average of
four to five departures per year to two or fewer, the financial impact would be substantial.

When Brian Mazur asked whether Shift Captains would serve as active participants in calls
rather than purely supervisory roles, Chief Selleck confirmed they would, functioning as a
member of the four-person crew while also managing incident command. He noted that this
dual role is operationally demanding, particularly in structure fires where an officer may
simultaneously be on a hose line, directing crew movements, and coordinating mutual aid on
the radio.

Chair Dubrulle asked whether the four new Shift Captain positions would require additional
command vehicles. Chief Selleck confirmed they would not, captains would ride on existing
apparatus.

Chief Selleck confirmed that cost estimates have been submitted to the Finance Director and
are still being refined as part of the FY2027 budget process. He committed to providing the
committee with a clear over/under comparison of the cost of the new positions versus current
overtime expenditures at the next budget presentation. He also noted that this proposal has
not yet been presented to the Select Board, and that the mechanism, whether a standard
budget line or a warrant article, has not yet been determined.
