New Business

Development of the Default Budget

Assistant Town Administrator Danielle Basora and School District Business Administrator
Doug Totten presented a slide show on the development of default budgets, covering the
statutory framework, how default budgets are calculated, and how adjustments are made. The
presentation noted that this topic had also been the subject of a state webinar earlier that day.

Assistant Town Administrator Basora outlined the jurisdictions under the Budget
Committee's purview: the Town of Goffstown (SB2), the Sewer Enterprise Fund, the
Goffstown School District (SB2), and the Goffstown and Grasmere Village Districts for
water. She noted that both water precincts remain under traditional town meetings and
therefore do not have default budgets. The SAU 19, which overlaps Goffstown and New
Boston, was also identified as a separate matter requiring its own form of contingency
budget, referred to as an "adjusted budget," governed by slightly different rules.

Chair Dubrulle explained that a significant legislative change, which took effect in August
2026, now mandates that official budget committees, rather than governing bodies, prepare
the default budget. She noted this represented an override of a previous Goffstown voter
decision to keep that responsibility with the governing body. Administrator Totten confirmed

this change is now written into statute and that the budget committee is fully responsible for

Administrator Totten explained the purpose of the default budget: in SB2 towns where voters
cast ballots rather than deliberate at a meeting, a contingency plan is needed if the operating
budget is voted down. The two contingency options are the default budget or a special
meeting. The special meeting option, while not requiring a court order, involves significant
cost and complexity, including new public hearings, ballot printing, and another round of
voting, which is why most towns opt for the default budget.

How the Default Budget Is Calculated

Administrator Totten referenced RSA 40:13, IX(b), which governs default budget
calculation. The default budget begins with the prior year's voter-approved appropriations
and is then adjusted, up or down, for debt service, legally mandated costs, and union
contracts approved by voters, while removing one-time expenditures and salaries for
eliminated positions.

Assistant Town Administrator Basora clarified that multi-year contracts entered into by the
governing body without a separate voter-approved warrant article cannot have their increases
carried into the default budget. The baseline approved amount is retained, but growth above
that level is not included. She referred to the "Sanborn" case as the legal precedent
establishing this principle. Chair Dubrulle reiterated the key point: for a contract's costs or
increases to appear in the default budget, voters must have explicitly approved that contract
through its own warrant article.

Members engaged in extended discussion to clarify the practical implications. It was
confirmed that COLA increases for non-union employees are not included in the default
budget, and that health insurance cost changes are only includable for employees covered by
voter-approved collective bargaining agreements (CBAs). Assistant Town Administrator
Basora noted that the town had historically been stricter on itself than required on some of
these items and would be adjusting this year based on updated legal guidance.

Members also discussed the impact on non-union employees, noting that in recent default
budget years, those employees effectively received pay cuts because their wage increases and
health insurance premium increases were not reflected in the default, requiring the Select
Board to reallocate funds from other lines through transfers.

Mandated Items and Adjustments

Assistant Town Administrator Basora outlined categories of items that can be adjusted in the
default budget because they are mandated by law, including New Hampshire Retirement
System contribution rates, workers' compensation, unemployment insurance, election costs,
solid waste disposal, annual financial audits, K–8 busing under RSA 189:6, elevator and
sprinkler inspections, and special education costs. She noted that the town would be newly
including solid waste tipping and disposal costs in the default, based on updated legal
analysis supporting their mandatory nature.

Administrator Totten noted that the DRA requires a documented justification for every
increase or decrease in the default budget, and that vague justifications such as "we think the

voters or a specific legal mandate.

DRA Form and Sign-Off Process

Assistant Town Administrator Basora walked through the DRA default budget form,
explaining that the prior year's adopted budget is automatically populated and is not editable.
Committees then enter increases or decreases with documented reasons, which produces the
default budget figure. She noted that a majority of budget committee members must sign the
default budget forms, just as they do for proposed budgets. She advised the committee not to
finalize and submit the DRA forms until ready for the January public hearings, to allow for
any legitimate changes in the intervening months.

Discussion on Budget Committee Authority and Collaboration

Chair Dubrulle raised concern about the implications of the committee now having authority
over the default budget. She noted that the committee could theoretically make policy-level
decisions, for example, declining to fund high school transportation in the default on the
grounds that it is not legally mandated, that would otherwise fall within the school board's
purview. She emphasized the need for close collaboration with the governing bodies and
careful navigation of the committee's expanded role.

Assistant Town Administrator Basora and Administrator Totten acknowledged the concern,
noting that the specificity required for any change to the default budget limits the practical
scope for policy-driven decisions, as justifications must be grounded in law or voter-
approved contracts.

Vice Chair Kearns asked whether materials presented to the committee could be formatted to
clearly distinguish which line items are fixed under the default framework so that the
committee does not spend significant time debating amounts they cannot change. Assistant
Town Administrator Basora confirmed that staff would flag those items and would redirect
discussion if the committee began deliberating on items outside its authority.


School Tax Cap with Doug

Administrator Totten provided an overview of House Bill 1300, now codified as RSA 32:5-I,
which establishes a school tax cap. The warrant article will appear on the November 2026
ballot, requiring 60% voter approval to take effect.

Two-Part Structure

If adopted, the law creates two distinct caps. The first limits SAU administrative costs to no
more than 6% of the total appropriated budget, excluding special education and facilities
costs. Administrator Totten noted that Goffstown's SAU costs currently represent
approximately 4% of the total budget even when those exclusions are not applied, meaning
this cap would not have a practical impact on the district at this time.

The second and more significant cap limits the amount that can be raised through local
property taxation for school purposes. This is not a direct cap on the total school budget, as
other revenue sources (state adequacy aid, tuition from New Boston, preschool tuition, etc.)
can still increase the budget beyond the cap. However, because local taxation represents the
largest share of school revenue, the cap effectively constrains the district's overall budget
growth.

Allowable Growth Factors

The local tax effort cap is permitted to increase by two factors: inflation and net new property
growth. Administrator Totten explained that the applicable inflation measure is tied to the
northeast regional index for the prior fiscal year, and that under the statutory language as
lawyers have interpreted it, the relevant period would be March 2027 inflation—a figure that
is not yet knowable. The DRA is charged with providing both the inflation adjustment and
the net new property growth figure, but no formal guidance has been received from the DRA
as to when those numbers will be available.

Administrator Totten acknowledged that Goffstown has seen meaningful property growth,
reflected in rising impact fees, but that the specific DRA-derived number remains
outstanding. Members noted the challenge of preparing a budget under a cap defined by
figures not yet available.

Override Provisions

The law provides that warrant articles passed by a 60% supermajority vote are excluded from
the tax cap calculation. Articles that pass with between 50% and 59.9% of the vote are
subject to the cap. Administrator Totten identified the pending GESS support staff contract as
one area where this distinction could become significant, noting the practical difficulty of
fulfilling a voter-approved contract if the tax cap does not allow sufficient revenue to fund it.
Legal counsel has indicated that if sufficient revenue is not available under the cap, the
district may have grounds to adjust the terms of a contract even if it passed, which
Administrator Totten noted was a significant and unresolved legal question.

Impact on Fiscally Conservative Districts

Administrator Totten emphasized that a tax cap would disproportionately affect towns like
Goffstown that have maintained low per-pupil costs. Unlike districts that have grown their
budgets more aggressively and could identify discretionary items to reduce, Goffstown has
limited slack and would face harder choices if constrained by the cap. He noted that
Goffstown's per-pupil cost is among the lowest in the region.

Chair Dubrulle noted the additional risk that if state adequacy aid were to decline, the cap
could prevent local taxpayers from making up the difference without triggering a 60%
override vote or a special meeting. Administrator Totten confirmed that this was a recognized
concern and that legal and legislative discussions are ongoing.

Members agreed that significant questions remain unanswered and that the committee would
revisit the topic, including at the joint meetings the following week, as more information
becomes available from the DRA and from legal counsel advising school districts statewide.

Proposal regarding meeting materials

Brian Mazur proposed that the committee request that departments and presenters provide
slide decks and supporting materials at least three business days in advance of meetings. He
noted that receiving materials the day of a meeting limits members' ability to prepare
meaningful questions and engage in productive deliberation.

General discussion followed. Chair Dubrulle indicated support for the proposal as a standing
request but cautioned against making it a binding rule, as some materials would be
legitimately unavailable in advance, for example, budget figures dependent on events
occurring close to a meeting date. Administrator Totten cited October 1st enrollment counts
as an upcoming example where advance delivery would not be feasible. Assistant Town
Administrator Basora expressed support for the request as a matter of professional practice.

Members discussed appropriate protocols around the receipt of advance materials, including
an informal understanding that members would not engage in public discussion of materials
prior to their formal presentation at a meeting, and that members would direct questions
through the committee meeting process rather than contacting department heads individually.

The committee reached consensus to adopt the three-business-day advance materials request
on a best-efforts basis, with no formal vote taken. The committee also agreed to request that
budget documents be provided in both printed and Excel formats.


Meeting procedures

Chair Dubrulle conveyed feedback received over the summer from members of the public
that the committee's meetings could be more efficient. She identified specific patterns to
address: excessive restatement of others' positions, extended discussion of minor budget line
items at the expense of major items, and insufficient preparation before meetings. She asked
members to come to meetings prepared, with specific questions and items for discussion
identified in advance and noted that the committee will be moving at a faster pace given the
increased workload from the default budget responsibility.

Chair Dubrulle also proposed a revised framework for how budget information is organized
and presented to the committee, structured around four categories: the default budget,
mandatory expenses not captured in the default (such as contracted obligations), status quo
costs (what it costs to simply repeat the prior year's operations), and new spending requests.
She acknowledged that the committee's budget software cannot generate this view
automatically and that the categorization would need to be developed manually, focusing on
major line items. The intent is to give both the committee and the public a clearer picture of
what is truly being requested and why.
