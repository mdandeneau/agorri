4. DISCUSSION WITH TRIDENT ON OWNER’S PROJECT MANAGER MODEL

The Trident Company was there to present on the Owner's Project Manager (OPM) model for
municipal facility projects. Gino Baroni, founder and managing principal of Trident Group, and
Marc Lehoullier, Owner’s Project Manager with Trident Group were present.

Mr. Baroni provided a history of the OPM role, explaining that as municipal projects became
increasingly complex, towns realized they could no longer rely on in-house staff, such as
Department of Public Works (DPW) directors, who already had full-time responsibilities. He
noted that projects in Massachusetts were frequently going over budget because municipalities
lacked dedicated experts to control costs, manage change orders, and guide the approval
process. He emphasized that an OPM is objectively responsible only to the town and the
taxpayers, distinguishing the role from a construction manager who builds the project for a
profit, and an architect who protects their own design interests and liability. Highlighting their
successful work in Salem, Mr. Baroni explained that after guiding seven consecutive school
projects to approval, the municipality hired Trident Group for its municipal buildings. Mr.
Lehoullier noted that they conducted a facilities assessment that prioritized a new police
department, which was currently being delivered under budget and on schedule. He explained
that nearly 100 percent of their projects use a construction management delivery method on a
completely open-book basis, where the construction manager receives a fee, general
conditions, and insurance reimbursements, while all other costs remain transparent.

Mr. Mr. Lehoullier stressed the importance of assembling the project team early to establish a
total project budget before bringing a warrant article to the voters. He detailed that a project's
total cost comprises three distinct elements:

- hard costs of construction, which go to the construction manager
- soft costs, including design fees, permitting, furniture, fixtures, equipment, and IT
- a reasonable contingency for owner-requested changes, unforeseen or concealed conditions
such as ledge, and weather mitigation for winter or hot weather

Mr. Lehoullier cited the Salem police station as an example of soft costs, noting that IT and low-

voltage security alone cost nearly $1.7 million. He explained that their goal is to ensure the
design follows the budget rather than the budget following the design. To achieve this, they
utilize three budgeting scenarios: conceptual, schematic design, design development, and a
final reconciliation for the guaranteed maximum price (GMP). Mr. Baroni added that
municipalities previously wasted money by funding 100 percent of design documents before
bidding, only to find bids exceeding the budget. Trident's strategy involves spending minimal
town funds to reach a conceptual or schematic design budget for the warrant article, stopping
design work until the project is officially approved.

The Committee discussed previously failed warrant articles for safety complexes. Mr. Swiniarski
noted that the Town didn’t fully understand why they failed. He suspected the 1st proposal's cost
lacked proper justification and the 2nd proposal, despite being significantly reduced, still faced
pushback over location, resulting in an approximate 60-40 vote. Chair Levesque added that the
sudden price drop left voters confused about the true need. Mr. Baroni shared a similar
experience with the Amherst School Department, which faced failed proposals of $60 million
and $50 million. He explained that Trident advised Amherst to abandon the old plans, rebrand
the project with a fresh look, and provide empirical data to justify the costs, rather than
presenting a recycled design to fatigued voters. Mr. Pieczarka noted that Amherst's rebranding
involved a 180-degree turn to reuse the majority of the existing structure to make additions less
impactful, which fundamentally changed the pricing structure and public perception.

Mr. Swiniarski raised the challenge of combating false information proliferating on social media,
noting the Town government historically lacked a dedicated person to monitor Facebook groups
during warrant article debates. Mr. Lehoullier and Mr. Baroni agreed that social media
misinformation must be monitored and addressed daily to prevent it from gaining traction. Mr.
Gilbert stated that the Town had monitored and responded to social media in the past, but the
primary issue was voter apathy and a reluctance to spend money due to property taxes and the
economic impacts of COVID-19. Voters often view police and fire services as out of sight and
out of mind until an emergency occurs, making them reluctant to fund the facilities proactively.
Chair Levesque emphasized that to overcome this, voters must clearly see the value and the
urgent need for the project.

Chair Levesque asked about the optimal organizational structure for a facilities committee. Mr.
Lehoullier described Salem's Municipal Building Advisory Committee (MBAC), which includes
administration, DPW, Police, Fire, Selectmen, Council representatives, and the Planning
Director, and is responsible for making recommendations to the Town Council. Mr. Baroni and
Chair Levesque agreed that department heads, such as the Police and Fire chiefs, should serve
in a non-voting advisory capacity to avoid the public perception that they are voting themselves
a new building. Mr. Baroni strongly recommended including a small advisory group of four or
five people, specifically including prominent community members and project naysayers, to
ensure public sentiment is understood and addressed. Mr. Gilbert confirmed that Bedford's
current committee operates as a recommending body to the Town Council who makes the final
decision on what to do. Chair Levesque stated how Police, Fire and DPW would come to their
meetings and tell the Committee what they need. Mr. Baroni added that public support from the
Police and Fire chiefs is essential, citing a 2017 safety complex project in another municipality
that failed entirely due to infighting between the Police and Fire departments. Chair Levesque
noted that Bedford's three Department Heads maintain an excellent, collaborative rapport.

Chair Levesque wanted to discuss a project timeline. Mr. Lehoullier stated that from their
perspective, they weren’t ready for a warrant. It has to be 2028. Chair Levesque outlined

Bedford's goal of bringing a warrant article forward in March 2027 to address the shared Police
and Fire building, which both departments have outgrown. She noted the first critical decision is
determining which department will vacate the current building, but expressed concern that
delaying a warrant article might signal to voters that the Town is doing nothing and the need is
not urgent. Mr. Baroni strongly cautioned against rushing a proposal for 2027, referencing
Amherst's successful decision to delay their vote to ensure they had a well-planned, rebranded
project backed by empirical data. He advised Bedford to use the upcoming year to establish the
project properly, gather data, and begin public presentations by August to avoid rushing during
the winter holidays. Chair Levesque stated that they get criticized for spending money on
designs. Mr. Swiniarski agreed that a 2027 article was unfeasible but worried about public
perception during the lag. Mr. Gilbert suggested consistently messaging to the public that the
Town is actively developing a responsible plan for 2028, rather than presenting an
unsubstantiated cost now. Mr. Baroni added that having 95 percent of the answers ready at
public meetings is what builds critical voter confidence.

Chair Levesque pointed out that once a warrant article passes, a new building is still two to
three years away, and the need is already overdue. Mr. Baroni estimated a delivery timeline of
24 to 32 months post-approval. Mr. Pieczarka stated a combined Police station and South River
Road Fire substation failed three years ago, and a pared-down substation proposal failed two
years ago. The Town possesses existing conceptual designs for relocating the Fire Department
centrally. Mr. Lianza discussed the lifespan of firehouses, noting Bedford's current station
opened in 1996 and is effectively 30 years behind the times given the Town's rapid population
doubling since then. He stressed the need to plan for the next 10 years of growth. Mr. Baroni
agreed, emphasizing that empirical data, such as heat maps and response times measured
against National Fire Association standards, must be used to justify facility locations and
demonstrate the urgent need to the public.

Chair Levesque asked what level of detail is included in a conceptual design for a warrant
article. Mr. Lehoullier explained that the design must go slightly beyond a basic concept to
ensure estimators can accurately capture hidden costs. He stated that mechanical, electrical,
and plumbing (MEP) narratives are necessary to outline the intended systems, such as efficient
mid-range options, so subcontractors can provide accurate pricing. Mr. Baroni clarified that this
detailed narrative is strictly for the estimators; the public presentation is simplified to a one-page
sheet showing hard costs, soft costs, and the owner's contingency, alongside schematics and
renderings, so voters know exactly what they are getting for the total project budget. Mr.
Lehoullier noted as a current example that Salem's upcoming 10,000-square-foot satellite
station is expected to cost around $10 million.

Chair Levesque asked how Trident structures its contracts and compensation. Mr. Baroni
explained that they do not act as the architect of record, engineer of record, or contractor, but
rather manage the process and mitigate risk on behalf of the owner. He stated that they reject
percentage-based fees, preferring to base compensation on the level of effort and duration.
Engagements typically begin on a time and reimbursable materials basis to understand the
project scope, and then convert to a monthly lump sum amount to provide the town with cost
certainty. He noted phases can be broken down into pre-bond vote, post-bond vote pre-
construction, construction administration, and closeout.

Mr. Sawyer thanked Mr. Baroni and Mr. Lehoullier for attending the meeting as a favor, without
compensation, to educate the Committee on the OPM approach. Chair Levesque saw the role
more as advisory/consultant, not project management. Mr. Baroni stated that they are project

5. REVIEW OF BEDFORD SAFETY COMPLEX: FIRE, POLICE, DPW ADMIN
         a. Key takeaways from tour

The Committee discussed observations and potential next steps following recent facility tours of
the Safety Complex.

Chair Levesque noted that Hollis has a 10,000-square-foot police department for 21 total staff
as a relative comparison. Mr. Gilbert clarified that the Bedford police building was built in 1994
when the staff was 28, whereas today the staff is at 65 or 67. Chair Levesque confirmed that
staffing for both Fire and Police had doubled. Mr. Gilbert added that fire calls had tripled, Police
calls had increased five-fold, and the Town population grew from 13,300 in 1994 to 24,000
today. He emphasized that everything had at least doubled or tripled except the building size,
which Chair Levesque agreed was the overall problem.

Chair Levesque shared her key takeaways from the Police tour she took with Councilor Bator,
which included:

- the overall safety of the building
- a lack of privacy and soundproof meeting space, noting she shares an office with four people
  and must leave to find space
- crowded office space
- storage for records and evidence spread everywhere
- Sally Port constraints preventing multiple events from happening simultaneously
- locker rooms beyond capacity, with some staff giving up lockers for new hires

6. DPW AND HIGHWAY GARAGE TOUR

Mr. Lianza commented on the highway garage tour, stating the facility is 20 years behind where
it should be. He observed that they maintain approximately 50 vehicles, including fire trucks,
and perform all repairs in a single bay. This requires lifting fire trucks and limits them to having
only one fire truck off the road at a time. He commended the Department's strategy of running
older trucks into the ground until they can be junked or used for parts, while holding new trucks
in reserve over time. Chair Levesque agreed with this approach, noting that the salvage value of
the older vehicles is very minimal. Mr. Lianza then raised safety concerns regarding employees
sleeping in the building during storms while vehicles are running. He noted that while there is
CO2 monitoring in the base and mezzanine areas, the building is not fit for sleeping. He
suggested that building a dormitory would be far safer and cheaper than building out the
existing facility to solve the problem, though he cautioned that dormitory plumbing issues do not
scale well over time. Chair Levesque joked that they should build near a hotel and rent rooms.

electricity to a room never designed for it is very expensive. He suggested mitigating the issue
by depopulating the building and moving administrative staff who do not require specialized
space into commercial properties. Chair Levesque outlined the options for the Town Office:
- renovate the existing building

- add onto what they have
- find another home and not keep the building

Mr. Pieczarka agreed that the lower floor has significant heat and air conditioning issues,
describing it as an underground cave with no open windows that requires careful air quality
monitoring. He noted that Peter keeps track of the air quality reports, but warned that spikes
could occur when more people are down there. Mr. Lianza stated that based on open-source
material, the building has reached the end of its 10-year design life, adding that the Town is
currently 30 years behind. Chair Levesque emphasized that Bedford is 20 to 30 years behind on
multiple facilities, not just the Town Office. Mr. Gilbert added that the Library opened in 1996,
and that was the last time the Town built anything.

Chair Levesque requested that DPW present at the next meeting on the 24th. Mr. Lianza
agreed to share his thoughts at that meeting. Chair Levesque stated that the first critical
decision will dictate how the rest of the dominoes fall. The single most important decision is who
stays and who goes at the safety complex. There have been past efforts to expand the safety
complex, noting they had not proven effective. Mr. Swiniarski and Mr. Lianza pointed out that
the complex cannot be built higher and there is limited space on the property. Chair Levesque
and Mr. Gilbert noted that expanding would require securing adjacent property, either through
eminent domain or paying a premium.

Mr. Lianza thought administrative staff could move to rented commercial office space while
preserving the Town Office building as the face of Bedford, where residents get dog tags and
licenses. Salem had to spend $4 million renovating a building to temporarily house police, and
argued that Bedford cannot afford temporary tent farms. Chair Levesque compared the Town
Office decision to homeowners choosing whether to renovate or buy new, emphasizing the
need to understand renovation costs before reaching a tipping point. Mr. Swiniarski asked about
the Town Office's usable square footage. Mr. Pieczarka estimated it at 10,000 square feet. Mr.
Sawyer estimated there are about 20 to 22 employees in the building, though it swells during
events like absentee voting.

Mr. Sawyer cautioned against moving staff off-site. The assessor, planner, clerk, and motor
vehicle registration must remain together for public convenience. Only IT, some finance, or HR
could potentially move, but HR needs to be near employees. Mr. Sawyer also corrected the
assumption that the upstairs is fine, stating that the building fails to meet current ADA standards
for public counters and lacks required nursing rooms. Chair Levesque agreed that building,
planning and Public Works administration should remain together for the public, though she
acknowledged an argument for moving Public Works administration near the highway garage.

Mr. Sawyer stated that making these decisions requires professionals on board, offering to
issue an RFP or RFQ for professional services if the Committee desired. Mr. Gilbert argued they
needed a game plan and cost estimates before talking to professionals. Chair Levesque favored
hiring professionals sooner to help with those exact decisions. Mr. Swiniarski considered hiring
an OPM for construction a no-brainer, comparing it to hiring someone to ensure painters do not
water down paint, because the government lacks an owner to oversee a project. Professionals
could provide a fresh start, noting that past designs might be wrong and identified needs might
need to be reined in. He used Salem as an example, stating that if a $30 million hard cost
building will never pass a vote, the need must change.

Chair Levesque disagreed with the Salem approach of asking for money piecemeal, stating that
Bedford residents want to know the complete plan and total cost upfront to avoid the other shoe

plan was a good idea. He emphasized that messaging should highlight the Town's growth

8. FACILITY HANDOUT AT SEPTEMBER 8TH ELECTION

Chair Levesque reported that four or five Councilors prepared and distributed a handout during
the recent voting day to share information with a larger segment of the population. She
explained that the flyer addressed how the Town would pay for new facilities, proposing an
approach similar to road bonds where facility bonds would be spread over 20 years and pulled
as needed when road bonds roll off. Out of approximately 4,500 voters, they handed out about
3,500 flyers, with about 20 people declining to take one. She stated that while a small number of
people expressed an inability or unwillingness to pay more taxes, the general reaction was very
positive, and many voters indicated they had supported the idea in the past. Councilor Greazzo
added that the information was very well received and that voters easily understood the analogy
to road bonds, appreciating that replacing a road bond with a facilities bond would keep
payments the same without a tax spike. Providing this information six months ahead of a
potential March warrant article was beneficial and suggested that a carefully crafted approach in
November could improve the chances for a fire substation warrant article. Plans already exist,
referencing plans mentioned by Mr. Pieczarka. Councilor Greazzo confirmed that the plans are
on paper and that Mr. Gilbert has a copy. He recalled the consultant's advice that such
materials need to be put in front of the public to help them make a decision. Chair Levesque
added that the consultants had cautioned the Committee to ensure they were fully prepared
before putting a warrant article forward. She agreed with this advice, stating that the Committee
would not proceed without consensus and sufficient momentum behind a proposal.

Mr. Gilbert commented on the flyer, advising that if the Committee is going to involve the
schools, they must explicitly communicate that the high school bond is also dropping off soon.
He emphasized that voters look at the total tax bill, so a reduction in school funding needs
should make the facilities decision easier. Chair Levesque noted that the Committee has tried to
collaborate with the School Board in the past to address mutual challenges. Mr. Gilbert stressed
the importance of understanding if the School Board plans to use their dropping bond money for
new projects, as both pieces combine into one tax bill. Chair Levesque observed that while
some residents understand there are two separate budgets, many still mistakenly believe the
Town Council controls the entire budget, with some even asking if she was running for the
School Board. She agreed with Mr. Gilbert that the School Board, Facilities Committee, and
Town Council must collaborate to build Town-wide consensus on the right path. Mr. Swiniarski
                     Bedford Facilities Committee – September 10, 2026 – page 7


pointed out that the dropping bonds represent tax revenue that different groups, including the
School District, will inevitably fight to use for their respective objectives.

Mr. Swiniarski addressed the fear of presenting voters with a previously rejected proposal for
2027, contrasting it with the risk that voters might think the facility problem has disappeared if no
article is presented. He suggested that framing the funding around dropping bonds so taxes do
not significantly increase is a good way to present the project as something new rather than the
same old request. Councilor Greazzo agreed that they must develop a new approach so the
issue does not lapse and be forgotten. He noted that the timing is perfect to take advantage of
the two bonds dropping off in 2027 and 2030, explaining that a fire substation bond in 2027
would replace a falling bond, and subsequent bonds could follow the same pattern seamlessly.
Chair Levesque shared a data point from the consultants regarding their recent work in
Amherst, where multiple failed attempts at a school project led them to take a fresh look rather
than tabling the issue. She emphasized that the Committee must communicate that they have
heard the voters and are taking a fresh approach, rather than doing nothing.

Councilor Greazzo requested a non-public meeting to discuss potential locations. Combining
identified properties with their existing building plans would bring them closer to a warrant
article. A new location and new bonding strategy would make it a different project than before.
Chair Levesque agreed to schedule a non-public session for the next meeting on September 24,
noting she wanted Vice Chair Bator and the full committee present for the discussion. Councilor
Greazzo advised members to look at maps and consider available properties in targeted areas
before the meeting, suggesting they think outside the box as properties with existing houses
could be purchased and demolished as long as they are in the right target zone and reasonably
priced. Chair Levesque joked about members donating their houses.

Councilor Greazzo asked if the Committee could consider issuing an RFP or an RFQ at the next
meeting to begin the process of hiring a consultant. Chair Levesque stated she wanted to do
homework first to avoid sole-sourcing and ensure a competitive bid by comparing at least one
other entity. She expressed concern about the transition from time-and-materials contracts to
lump-sum or flat-sum contracts across different phases, questioning what level of consultant
availability a lump-sum payment guarantees. Councilor Greazzo expressed support for the
phased approach mentioned by the consultants, noting that the Town would not be obligated to
the finish line if a bond fails. One individual agreed, and Mr. Sawyer confirmed there would be
no more payments in that scenario. Mr. Pieczarka added that it becomes a lump sum once
secured, and Councilor Greazzo noted the built-in reliability of consistent payments until
completion.

Chair Levesque asked if they could obtain scrubbed sample contracts to better understand the
engagement models. Mr. Swiniarski suggested obtaining the public contracts from Salem or
Amherst, and Mr. Gilbert agreed, comparing it to the public contract signed with Fulcrum. Mr.
Swiniarski noted they could submit a Right-to-Know request if necessary. Councilor Greazzo
pointed out that an RFP or RFQ would reveal the costs based on required tasks, though he
cautioned they might only receive one response. Mr. Swiniarski stated that the RFP could
dictate the phase structure. Chair Levesque agreed that the RFP must clearly identify the
phases and require time and expenses to ensure an apples-to-apples comparison. She offered
to draft an RFP for the Committee to review with the anticipated sample contracts. Mr.
Swiniarski cautioned that the details of subsequent phases cannot be fully known until the first
phase is complete. Chair Levesque agreed they are still in the stage of not knowing what they
do not know.
