           Meeting. Time will be divided equally among those wishing to speak, however,
           no person will be allowed to speak for more than 5 minutes.
        2. No person may address the council more than twice on any issue in any
           meeting. Comments must be addressed to the Chair and must not be personal
           or derogatory about any other person.

Stride Together: Hooksett Track & Wellness Project

Submitted By:

Samera Dalal – MSPM Candidate

University of New Hampshire

Date:

August 19, 2025

1. Business Overview / Background

The Hooksett town running track is a highly valued public asset used by residents of all ages for

walking, running, and gathering. However, the current condition of the track and surrounding

infrastructure poses safety concerns, lacks accessibility for individuals with disabilities, and fails

to fully realize the potential of the space as a community wellness hub.

The Stride Together project proposes a comprehensive enhancement of the Hooksett track area

to address these gaps. The project focuses on three key goals:

        •   Safety: Installing protective fencing along the edge nearest to the active railway line.

        •   Accessibility: Constructing ADA-compliant pathways using gravel and

            steppingstones.

        •   Wellness: Incorporating new benches, a strength-training zone, and pull-up/dip

            stations to support diverse outdoor fitness activities.

By improving safety, inclusivity, and wellness-focused design, this initiative aims to modernize

the facility and foster long-term community engagement.

2. Statement of the Business Opportunity

There is a unique opportunity to:

        •   Increase public safety through physical barriers near hazardous zones.

       •   Launch and manage the Hooksett Youth Running Club.

BACKGROUND INFORMATION:
  2026 Health Insurance Renewal Rates
  HealthTrust is the benefits advisor team for Anthem (medical) and Delta (dental) insurance plans
  for the Town of Hooksett. HealthTrust representative Debie Clayton, is a scheduled appointment
  for the Council's October 22nd meeting for Q&A on CY2026 health insurance renewal rates and
  discussion of an additional retiree medical plan option.

   Overall rate increase of 14.5%
    Access Blue AB201DED = 10.7%
    Anthem Blue ABSOS20/40 = 15.4%
    Lumenos = 10.1%

  Dental Insurance increase is 4.4%

  Last fiscal year the Town paid $2,394,649 in Health Insurance from the General Fund. This number
  includes Insurance opt out and HRA reimbursements. The CY2026 rates at 14.5% are projected to
  be an increase of $377,565. Costs are based on current enrollments and subject to change based

    How Your Rates
    Are Determined

HealthTrust, a nonprofit public risk pool in New Hampshire, has been providing benefits and services to public sector
employers for more than 40 years. We strive to set rates that are accurate, meaning the rates set for the upcoming
plan year represent our actuary’s best estimate of the amount of contributions needed to cover expected costs of
claims and administration, and other expenses, as well as the amount necessary to replenish or maintain the capital
adequacy reserve at the target level. As the industry changes and evolves, HealthTrust continues to outperform
benchmarks based on a variety of performance indicators, delivering robust benefits at a lower cost than its
competitors and the private insurance market.

Rating Process
What it means for Member Groups: Your contribution covers expected costs.

HealthTrust uses data-driven, transparent, actuarially determined processes to set the rates needed to cover claims,
reserves, and administration. HealthTrust’s actuary performs an annual calculation using actual incurred claims;
data supplied by service providers; projected costs of fees, taxes, and administration; and the amount necessary to
replenish or rebuild the capital adequacy reserve. After public hearings and internal review, the HealthTrust board
sets the final rates, which it then communicates to Member Groups.

To further enhance the rating process, with this rating cycle HealthTrust has started to use the number of enrolled
individuals instead of the number of eligible individuals when determining the size of a rating entity for underwriting
purposes, further improving rating accuracy. The size of a rating entity is used to determine credibility weighting,
which is a key pooling mechanism used to set rates for groups of varying sizes that are fair, stable, and actuarially
sound. Credibility weighting balances individual group experience with the broader pool’s claims experience based
on the number of enrolled individuals in a rating entity. Groups with 50 or fewer enrolled individuals are too small
for their experience to be credible, and for each renewal, these small groups are placed together into a single rating
entity referred to as the 50 and Under Group.

Capital Risk Charge
What it means for Member Groups: HealthTrust builds sufficient reserves to pay unexpected
future claims.

The rates you pay cover projected claims and expenses. However, at times, claims can exceed projections. In this
case, the capital adequacy reserve covers unexpected future claims and expenses. In years when HealthTrust’s
net position is below the capital adequacy target, such as during the years following the COVID-19 pandemic,
HealthTrust may add a reasonable capital risk charge to overall rates in order to replenish the capital adequacy
reserve. The capital adequacy reserve successfully covered significantly increased claims during the post-COVID
claims spike and is being steadily rebuilt through the capital risk charge resulting in a $10 million gain in FY2024, a
$9.5 million gain in FY2025, and a projected $13.3 million gain in the current year, FY2026.

Non-Assessable Coverage
What it means for Member Groups: HealthTrust does not issue assessments.

HealthTrust’s non-assessable coverage means your contributions (premiums) are the limit of your exposure and we
will not require you to pay additional assessments. This protects you, and your taxpayers, from unexpected mid-year
bills and year-end assessments. If claims exceed contributions, HealthTrust is responsible for any losses that may
occur and will replenish through prospective rate adjustments in future renewals.

Slide 4 – What is an SB2 Town?

Talking Points:

       Deliberative Session – Held first, usually in early February.
   •   Residents discuss, amend, and finalize the wording of warrant articles (only monetary
       warrant articles) (including the budget).
   •   No binding votes on passing the articles are taken at this session—just on the
       text/amounts.
       Voting Day Held later (Town Election Day in March).
   •   All finalized warrant articles are voted on by secret ballot, allowing people to participate
       without having to attend a long meeting.
       Key Differences from Traditional Town Meeting:
   •   Traditional Town Meeting: discussion and voting happen in the same session, and you
       must be present to vote.
   •   SB2: discussion and amendments happen at the deliberate session; the actual yes/no votes
       happen later at the polls.
       Purpose:
       SB2 was designed to increase voter participation by allowing secret ballot voting at the
       polls rather than requiring attendance at a multi-hour meeting.

       voice into every meeting and decision.
   •   Supporting the Council’s work is the Town Administrator, who manages the day-to-day
       operations of the town. Think of the Town Administrator as the CEO of the town’s

       library services. Appointed by the Council or other officials, these board members bring

Slide 6- How Decisions are Made

Talking Points:

   •   Decisions in our town often begin with an idea — and those ideas can come from many
       places. Sometimes residents bring forward concerns or suggestions because they see a
       need in the community. Other times, town staff or department heads identify issues or
       opportunities based on their day-to-day work. And of course, our elected officials may
       propose ideas based on their vision or feedback from constituents.
   •   Once an idea is introduced, it usually moves to one of the town’s boards or committees
       for a closer look. These groups are made up of residents and experts who study the issue

   •   In this way, decision-making in local government is a process of listening, learning, and
       collaborating — bringing together ideas from across the community to create solutions
       that work for everyone.

Slide 8- How is the Tax Rate Set?
Talking Points:

   •   Each fall, the Department of Revenue Administration (DRA) certifies property tax rates
       for every municipality. The rate is based on the tax levy—the amount that must be raised
       through property taxes after subtracting all other revenues (state aid, motor vehicle fees,
       permits, investments, etc.) from the town’s adopted budget. If revenues come in higher
       than expected, the tax rate decreases; if revenues fall short, taxes make up the difference.
   •   By law, tax bills must show the property’s assessed value and the rates for each portion of
       the tax (municipal, local and state education, county, and village district if applicable).
       Most towns receive their certified rates by mid-November and issue bills due in
       December. Ultimately, the property tax rate is driven by how much money must be raised
       and is apportioned based on property values.

Slide 9 - Union Negotiations
Talking Points:
       In most New Hampshire towns (and generally in local government), union negotiations
       are handled under the state’s public sector labor law — the Public Employee Labor
       Relations Board (PELRB) rules — and follow a structured process.
   •   Here’s the typical flow for a municipal union contract negotiation:
   1. Parties Involved

   •   Union – Represents the employees (e.g., police, fire, highway, or clerical bargaining
       units).
   •   Town Negotiating Team – Usually made up of:
   •   Town Administrator or Town Manager
   •   Finance Director (sometimes)
   •   One or more members of the Town Council (appointed as authorized negotiators)
   •   Sometimes an outside labor attorney or HR consultant for expertise and neutrality.
   2. Negotiation Process
       Preparation
   •   Both sides review the existing Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) and gather data
       (salaries, benefits, comparable towns, grievances, etc.).
   •   Town’s governing body sets parameters for negotiations (e.g., budget limits, benefit
       changes).
       Bargaining Sessions
   •   Held privately between the union and the town’s negotiating team.


                                                                                                      Page 63 of 117
                                                                                         Agenda Item #16.1.


   •   Discussions cover wages, hours, working conditions, benefits, and any contract language
       changes.
   •   Both sides can propose changes and counterproposals.
       Tentative Agreement (TA)
   •   When both sides agree on terms, they sign a tentative agreement.
   •   The TA then goes to:
   •   Union members for a ratification vote.
   •   Town governing body (Council) for approval.
Monetary component of the contract goes to the voters as Warrant Articles.
Slide 10 – Non-Union Employees

Talking Points:

   •   Coverage: Non-union employees are managed under the Town’s Personnel Plan, which
       outlines policies for compensation, benefits, and working conditions.
   •   Pay Adjustments: Salary and hourly wage increase for these employees are tied to the
       Personnel Plan. Adjustments are typically based on factors such as market comparisons,
       cost-of-living adjustments (COLA), and recommendations from Administration.
   •   Proposal Process: Each year, the Town Administrator reviews the Personnel Plan and
       may propose updates—this can include adjustments to wage scales or benefits to ensure
       competitiveness and fairness.
   •   Town Council Review: Proposed changes are presented to the Town Council for
       discussion and approval. The Council evaluates the fiscal impact, consistency with
       policy, and alignment with comparable municipalities.
   •   Budget Inclusion: Once approved by the Council, any financial changes (such as wage
       increases) are incorporated into the proposed operating budget or as a separate warrant
       article.
   •   Voter Approval: Because the Personnel Plan affects taxpayer funding, the final budget—
       containing any wage adjustments for non-union employees—is brought forward at the
       annual Town Meeting/ballot for voter approval.
Slide 11– How is the Town Budget is Created

Talking Points:

   •   Creating the town’s budget is a yearly process that balances community needs with
       available resources.
   •   Department Requests – Each town department submits a proposed budget and warrant
       articles for the upcoming year, outlining what they need to maintain services and any new
       projects they’d like to pursue. (July)

                                                                                          Agenda Item #16.1.


   •   Town Administrator Review – The Town Administrator reviews all requests with the
       departments, adjusts, and prepares a draft budget. (July – August)
   •   Town Council Review – The draft is presented to the Town Council, which discusses
       priorities, makes changes, and ensures the budget is balanced. (September)
   •   Budget Committee Review – Town council sends the budget to the Budget Committee.

       multiple stages.

Slide 15 – TIF District
   •   Definition: A TIF District is a tool that lets the Town reinvest a portion of new tax

       the tax rate for everyone—it just redirects the growth in taxes from development.
   •   Purpose: The main goal is to encourage economic development and make needed
       infrastructure improvements without shifting the burden onto the general taxpayer. It
       helps the Town stay competitive in attracting businesses and improving key areas.
   •   How It Works:
           o When the TIF is created, the property values in the district are “frozen” as the
                baseline.
           o As new development happens, property values increase.
           o The additional tax revenue from that increase—the increment—is captured and
                set aside for improvements within the district.
           o The base taxes still continue to go into the Town’s general fund, so there’s no loss
                of existing revenue.
   •   Eligible Uses: The funds can be used for public infrastructure like roads, water and sewer
       lines, sidewalks, streetlights, and landscaping. They can also be used for things that
       support development, such as parking or utility upgrades.
   •   Oversight: The Town Council manages the TIF District and ensures funds are used
       appropriately. In some cases, a TIF Advisory Board may be established to make
       recommendations. Each year, there’s a required public report on the district’s financial
       activity.
   •   Benefit to Residents: Over time, this approach helps grow the tax base, improve
       infrastructure, and attract private investment—leading to long-term benefits for the entire
       community without raising overall tax rates.

Title:              RSA 72:81 Re-Adoption - "Commercial and Industrial Exemption Statewide"
Meeting:            Town Council - 22 Oct 2025
Department:         Community Development
Staff Contact:      Grant McGregor, Town Planner

BACKGROUND INFORMATION:
Per RSA 72:82, II, A vote adopting the "Commercial and Industrial Exemption Statewide" remains in effect for a maximum
of 5 tax years. For any application which has already been granted prior to expiration the 5-tax-year-period, the exemption
continues until it expires. The Town Council adopted the RSA 72:81 Property Tax Exemption in March 2021, thereby
making it effective on April 1, 2021.

If not renewed prior to April 1, 2026, the exemption will be discontinued until the time of future adoption. No applications
would be eligible for the 2026 tax year because the exemption opportunity would no longer be available. That is why it is
important to address the re-adoption of the incentive prior to the end of 2025. All applications for an exemption effective
starting in the upcoming tax year must be submitted prior to December 31. One property owner has expressed interest in
applying prior to the end of 2025.

RSA 72:80 - 72:83 describes the "Commercial and Industrial Construction Exemption Statewide". This series of 4 statutes
is broken down into 4 sections.
1. Definitions.
     1. Defines any NH Town as eligible to offer this exemption.
     2. Defines "commercial uses" and "industrial uses" as makes common sense.
2. Description of local taxes exempted
     1. The adoption (or renewal/re-adoption) must be by vote of the Council per procedure for adoption of ordinances.
     2. The exemption applies to municipal and local school property taxes.
     3. The exemption shall be a specified percentage on an annual basis of the increase in assessed value attributable
        to construction of new structures, and additions, renovations, or improvements to existing structures, not
        exceeding 50 percent per year.
     4. The exemption may run for a maximum period of 10 years following the new construction.
     5. Explains that the percentage rate and duration of the exemption shall be granted on a per case basis based on
        the amount and value of public benefit as determined by the Council.
3. Procedure for adoption of the exemption.
     1. The entire Town can be eligible to receive the exemption or only select properties.
     2. Explains that public benefit shall be defined by the Council as part of the adoption of the property tax exemption.
     3. Explains that the Council must specify the maximum percentage of new assessed value to be exempted, the
        maximum number of years duration of the exemption following new construction, a definition of public benefit
        under the limitations under #2(3-4) (above).
     4. The exemption shall take effect in the tax year beginning April 1 following its adoption.
     5. A vote adopting the described exemption remains in effect for a maximum of 5 tax years. Any application which
        has already been granted prior to expiration the 5-tax-year-period, the exemption shall continue until it expires.
4. Procedure for application for the exemption.
     1. An owner shall apply for the exemption under RSA 72:81 prior to construction, but not after December 31 before
        the beginning of the tax year for which the exemption is sought.

FINANCIAL IMPACT:
Where AV is used in this report, it means "Assessed Value".

The incentive has not been utilized to its fullest potential. One property owner has been awarded the
Commercial/Industrial Exemption in the 5-years the program has been active. Several property owners were not made
aware of the opportunity to apply for this exemption until after construction, leaving them ineligible. At the August 2025
EDAC meeting, EDAC recommended that the maximum exemption period be extended to 7-years and 30% of the post-
improvement AV. The 2021 approval was for maximums of 5-years and 25% of the post-improvement AV.

It is important to note that each application for exemption must be granted/denied by the Town Council before 2/28 and is
effective on 4/1 if granted. The first year of the exemption is essentially always wasted for this reason. Starting site work
would be difficult on frozen ground and there is no financial benefit in being taxed for a structure that cannot be occupied.

BACKGROUND INFORMATION:
Attached is the history of the Unassigned General Fund Balance. As of June 30, 2025 the Town has
$5,610,145.

“In government accounting, fund balance is the difference between operating assets and liabilities
resulting in a surplus or a deficit. A common misconception is that fund balance is a cash account,
associated with or correlated to a government’s bank account balance. But unlike a personal bank
account, a general fund balance is not a “cash-account;” it is a measure of equity between revenues
and expenditures.” Katherine Heck, Government Finance Advisor for NHMA.

The Town's Unassigned Fund Balance Policy is to increase the minimum growth by .75% each year
before applying an up to level of $300,000 to the tax rate until the unassigned fund balance reaches
the 8% target balance. It is essential that the Town maintains an adequate level of unassigned fund
balance to mitigate financial risk that can occur from emergencies and economic downturns.

We are still waiting on NH Department of Revenue to review information with regards to setting the
tax rate.


FINANCIAL IMPACT:
Unassigned fund balance used to off-set taxes lowers the tax rate.
