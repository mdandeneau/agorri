Slide 4 – What is an SB2 Town?

Talking Points:

       Deliberative Session – Held first, usually in early February.
   •   Residents discuss, amend, and finalize the wording of warrant articles (only monetary
       warrant articles) (including the budget).
   •   No binding votes on passing the articles are taken at this session—just on the
       text/amounts.
       Voting Day Held later (Town Election Day in March).
   •   All finalized warrant articles are voted on by secret ballot, allowing people to participate
       without having to attend a long meeting.
       Key Differences from Traditional Town Meeting:
   •   Traditional Town Meeting: discussion and voting happen in the same session, and you
       must be present to vote.
   •   SB2: discussion and amendments happen at the deliberate session; the actual yes/no votes
       happen later at the polls.
       Purpose:
       SB2 was designed to increase voter participation by allowing secret ballot voting at the
       polls rather than requiring attendance at a multi-hour meeting.
Slide 5 – Structure of Town Government
Talking Points:

   •   At the heart of our town’s government is the Town Council — the legislative body that
       acts much like the town’s “board of directors.” The Council is responsible for setting
       overall policies, approving the annual budget, and representing the interests of all
       residents. Council members are elected by you, the voters, so they carry the community’s
       voice into every meeting and decision.
   •   Supporting the Council’s work is the Town Administrator, who manages the day-to-day
       operations of the town. Think of the Town Administrator as the CEO of the town’s

       government, working closely with department heads—like public works, police, parks,
       and more—to make sure the Council’s policies and goals are carried out effectively and
       efficiently.
   •   In addition to elected officials, our town relies on many appointed boards and
       committees. These groups handle everything from planning and zoning to recreation and
       library services. Appointed by the Council or other officials, these board members bring
       expertise and community perspectives to help guide important decisions.
   •   Together, elected officials, the Town Administrator, and appointed boards form a system
       designed to balance community input, professional management, and specialized
       knowledge — all to serve the best interests of our town.
Slide 6- How Decisions are Made

Talking Points:

   •   Decisions in our town often begin with an idea — and those ideas can come from many
       places. Sometimes residents bring forward concerns or suggestions because they see a
       need in the community. Other times, town staff or department heads identify issues or
       opportunities based on their day-to-day work. And of course, our elected officials may
       propose ideas based on their vision or feedback from constituents.
   •   Once an idea is introduced, it usually moves to one of the town’s boards or committees
       for a closer look. These groups are made up of residents and experts who study the issue
       in detail, gather information, and discuss the potential impacts. They then make
       recommendations to the Town Council or other decision-making bodies. This step
       ensures that decisions are thoughtful, balanced, and informed by a variety of
       perspectives.
   •   Before any final decisions are made, the town often holds public hearings — these are
       your chance to speak up, ask questions, and share your thoughts. Public hearings are an
       essential part of transparency and community involvement, giving everyone a voice
       before policies, projects, or budgets are approved.
   •   In this way, decision-making in local government is a process of listening, learning, and
       collaborating — bringing together ideas from across the community to create solutions
       that work for everyone.

Slide 8- How is the Tax Rate Set?
Talking Points:

   •   Each fall, the Department of Revenue Administration (DRA) certifies property tax rates
       for every municipality. The rate is based on the tax levy—the amount that must be raised
       through property taxes after subtracting all other revenues (state aid, motor vehicle fees,
       permits, investments, etc.) from the town’s adopted budget. If revenues come in higher
       than expected, the tax rate decreases; if revenues fall short, taxes make up the difference.
   •   By law, tax bills must show the property’s assessed value and the rates for each portion of
       the tax (municipal, local and state education, county, and village district if applicable).
       Most towns receive their certified rates by mid-November and issue bills due in
       December. Ultimately, the property tax rate is driven by how much money must be raised
       and is apportioned based on property values.

Slide 9 - Union Negotiations
Talking Points:
       In most New Hampshire towns (and generally in local government), union negotiations
       are handled under the state’s public sector labor law — the Public Employee Labor
       Relations Board (PELRB) rules — and follow a structured process.
   •   Here’s the typical flow for a municipal union contract negotiation:
   1. Parties Involved

   •   Union – Represents the employees (e.g., police, fire, highway, or clerical bargaining
       units).
   •   Town Negotiating Team – Usually made up of:
   •   Town Administrator or Town Manager
   •   Finance Director (sometimes)
   •   One or more members of the Town Council (appointed as authorized negotiators)
   •   Sometimes an outside labor attorney or HR consultant for expertise and neutrality.
   2. Negotiation Process
       Preparation
   •   Both sides review the existing Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) and gather data
       (salaries, benefits, comparable towns, grievances, etc.).
   •   Town’s governing body sets parameters for negotiations (e.g., budget limits, benefit
       changes).
       Bargaining Sessions
   •   Held privately between the union and the town’s negotiating team.

   •   The TA then goes to:

Slide 10 – How is the Town Budget is Created

Talking Points:

   •   Creating the town’s budget is a yearly process that balances community needs with
       available resources.
   •   Department Requests – Each town department submits a proposed budget and warrant
       articles for the upcoming year, outlining what they need to maintain services and any new
       projects they’d like to pursue. (July)
   •   Town Administrator Review – The Town Administrator reviews all requests with the
       departments, adjusts, and prepares a draft budget. (July – August)
   •   Town Council Review – The draft is presented to the Town Council, which discusses
       priorities, makes changes, and ensures the budget is balanced. (September)
   •   Budget Committee Review – Town council sends the budget to the Budget Committee.
   •   Public Input – Residents are encouraged to attend public hearings and share their
       thoughts before the budget is finalized. (September – January – Town Council meetings
       and Budget Committee meetings)
   •   Deliberative Session – The Budget Committee finalizes the budget, and it is put to the
       voters for discussion.
   •   Town Meeting (Voting) – All voters are encouraged to get our and vote in March for the
       budget and warrant articles. We encourage all to be informed before going to the ballot
       boxes by providing public input, and/or reviewing the Deliberative Session guide and the
       Voter’s Guide provided by the town prior to voting.
   •   Key Point: The process is collaborative, transparent, and open to public participation at
       multiple stages.

•   Why do projects take so long?
       o Projects in local government go through several phases, and each one is important
           for safety, quality, and accountability:
       o Planning & Design – Engineers and staff determine the best approach, materials,
           and timeline.
       o Funding – Money may come from the town budget, grants, or state/federal funds.
           Sometimes we need to wait for funding to be approved or released.
       o Permitting & Approvals – We follow state and federal regulations,

       o Public Input – Residents get the chance to review and comment before work
           begins.
       o Bidding & Contractor Selection – By law, projects must be competitively bid to
           get the best value for taxpayers.
       o Construction & Weather Factors – Work can be delayed by weather, material
           shortages, or contractor schedules.
       o While it can feel slow, these steps help ensure the final project is safe, cost-
           effective, and meets community needs.
