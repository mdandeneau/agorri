Dear Town Administrator and Town Officials,

The Hooksett Old Home Day Committee is pleased to confirm that we have secured
sponsorship funding for a professional fireworks display as part of the 2026 Hooksett Old
Home Day celebration on Saturday, September 19, 2026.

The display will be conducted by JPI Pyrotechnics, LLC, a professional licensed fireworks
company. The current plan is for a conventional, non-musical fireworks display beginning at
approximately 8:30 PM, utilizing the football field at Donati Park as the launch area, consistent
with the location used for fireworks displays in previous years.

JPI is currently preparing the required permit and insurance documentation. Because Hooksett
Old Home Day is not a Town-sponsored event and the Old Home Day Committee does not own
the field, JPI requires written authorization from an appropriate Town official granting
permission to use the football field and necessary surrounding area for the fireworks
display. This authorization will be included as part of JPI's permit package submitted to the
appropriate state and local authorities.

We understand that final approval of the fireworks display is subject to all applicable fire, safety,
permitting, and site requirements. We will coordinate directly with the Hooksett Fire Department,
Police Department, and other Town departments as necessary regarding the required safety
perimeter, access, setup, fire detail, crowd control, and any other conditions established for the
display.

JPI's current plan calls for aerial shells up to 4 inches, subject to approval by the Hooksett Fire
Department and the available safety distances at the site. JPI has indicated that the site may
potentially accommodate shells up to 5 inches; however, we would only consider that option if
the Fire Department determines it can be safely and appropriately accommodated.

At this time, we respectfully request written confirmation from the Town granting the
Hooksett Old Home Day Committee and JPI Pyrotechnics permission to utilize the
football field and necessary surrounding Town property for the professional fireworks
display on Saturday, September 19, 2026, at approximately 8:30 PM.

Because JPI needs to submit its permit documentation to the Fire and Police Departments as
soon as possible, we would greatly appreciate receiving the Town's written authorization at its
earliest convenience.

We are excited to bring fireworks back as a special conclusion to this year's Old Home Day
celebration and appreciate the Town's assistance and continued support of this community
tradition.

Thank you,Carrie Hyde

                                Part One: Native American History

         The land which now makes up New Hampshire was formed over millions of years. A
combination of volcanic eruptions and four separate ice ages, followed by warming periods, gave
birth to swaths of temperate forests, freshwater basins, and the granite-rich mountains which give
the state its nickname.1 Nestled in the bosom of the Merrimack Valley is the town of Hooksett,
whose borders contain Hooksett Falls (or, as the early European settlers referred to it,
Annahooksett, meaning “at the place of beautiful trees”2), an ideal location for human settlement
and prosperity. The earliest people to inhabit the area were the Paleo-Indians,3 who arrived at
least 13,000 years ago.4 They did not build permanent settlements and followed caribou herds for
hunting. The climatic shifts following the last ice age led to changes in the region’s flora and
fauna, with an increase in biodiversity, which allowed people to stop following herds and instead
create seasonal settlements to take advantage of the fishing and hunting opportunities and trade
with other communities. Around 1,000 BCE, people began to develop agriculture and grew corn,
beans, and squash. Native populations increased and formalized into the tribes recognized today.5
         While there are no federally recognized indigenous tribes in New Hampshire at this time,
it is largely accepted that the Hooksett area was primarily the territory of the Pennacook, who
belonged to the Algonquian language family.6 Here the Merrimack River, called N’amaskik or
Namoskeag (from which is derived the name Amoskeag, meaning “place of many fish”) by the
indigenous residents, formed the rich fishing grounds and fertile soil now called Annahooksett
Falls.7 The word “Pennacook” is of dubious origin. It could be derived from the Algonquian
word Pennaquiauke, meaning “crooked place,” which was the name given to the region in
present-day Concord where the Merrimack River formed a dramatic crook. It could also be
derived from P-negokw, meaning sandhill; near Annahooksett Falls was a hill called Sugar Ball
Bluff by the English, where the Pennacook had a fort. English settlers used “Pennacook” to refer
to all indigenous inhabitants of the Merrimack Valley because it was the most dominant tribe in
the region. There were other tribes in the area, such as the Nashua, Souhegan, Squamscot, and
Piscataqua; these later merged into the Pennacook Confederacy.8
         Prior to the seventeenth century, life in Hooksett was relatively cyclical. In spring and
fall, people traveled to the nearby Amoskeag Falls to take advantage of the fishing season.
Salmon headed upstream to spawn, while shad, alewife, and lamprey populated the calm pools
between the sharp drops of the falls. At the peak of fishing season, it is said that there were so

        Because the land surrounding the Amoskeag was unsuitable for farming, the Pennacook
traveled to Annahooksett Falls to sow their crops.11 They cleared land for farming through a
process called “girdling,” in which something is fastened about a tree to stop the flow of sap,
causing the tree to die and making it much easier to cut down.12 They primarily grew corn, beans,
melons, gourds, pumpkins, squash, and tobacco. They also gathered acorns, walnuts, chestnuts,
and berries from the surrounding forest.13 After the harvest, people spent the late autumn into
early winter hunting moose, deer, and caribou.14 They hunted with arrows, spears, snares,
tomahawks,15 and dogs.16 In winter, they utilized snowshoes to travel and returned to their
headquarters near present-day Concord, where they stored many of their provisions.17
        Before European contact, men were the primary growers of food. Women were the
primary cooks and cleaned and prepared meat by cutting it into strips for drying. It could be
cooked by boiling in a kettle, roasting on a fork, or broiled in a hole filled with hot stones. A
common dish was to add heated stones to a wooden trough filled with water to force it to a boil,
then add beans and corn. This was sometimes served with meat or bread. Bread was created
using maize flour and baking the dough on flat stones or on leaves underneath hot ashes.18
        The tools they used for cooking were primarily made of shells or bone.19 Tools for other
purposes were typically made of stone or wood. Blades intended for heavier labor would be
shaped by grinding, while those used for more specialized tasks were chipped into fine points
through a process called flaking.20 Local stone, such as granite, was quarried nearby, but they
also used materials like jasper and soapstone which were not found locally.21 The presence of
tools made of non-native stones found in archaeological sites around Hooksett is evidence of the
complex trading routes that existed throughout New England.22

         Archaeological evidence also shows that people were creating ceramic vessels as early as
3,300 years ago. Women were the primary potters and crafters.23 They created unglazed pots and
used sticks to carve designs into the wet clay before it set. Some of them were decorated with
beads and paint. Women also crafted clothing and moccasins from animal skins and furs and
adorned them with beads, feathers, and shells. They created jewelry from wampum, a polished
shell which was also used as currency. Some of their other creations include woven baskets and
mats, colorful beads, snowshoes, and utensils made from bone, shell, wood, or stone.24
         They lived in wigwams, which were constructed by driving flexible saplings into the
ground in a circle, fastening them at the top with a hole for smoke to escape, and covering them
with barks or skins. The floor was lined with rush or bark mats. A central pole kept the structure
together and served as the heart of the home. It was used to hold items like kettles or fish and at
the bottom were stones for a fire.25 People lived in closely grouped bands of about 50-200
people. Individuals also traveled frequently and created paths which are still followed today.26
Their primary mode of transportation was by water. Quick travel was conducted in canoes made
of wooden frames covered in birch bark and sealed with pitch. They were light enough to carry
over land when necessary. Heavy dugouts for more serious travel were made by burning or
carving massive tree trunks.27
         People frequently married outside their immediate bands, creating a complex kin
network.28 They practiced both monogamy and polygamy, and though divorce was rare, it was
not unusual nor culturally frowned upon. Mothers were the primary caregivers to children. They
tied their babies to a tkinogan, which they wore on their backs or hung from a tree or wigwam
pole while they worked. Respect for one’s parents was deeply ingrained in their culture.29 Their
religious beliefs centered around the idea that everything has a spiritual force. Gathering food
was an especially spiritual practice, and so the river was significant in their beliefs and many of
their most important ceremonies took place in or near rivers.30 Evidence suggests that
Annahooksett Falls was one of the major gathering places for the Pennacooks to conduct
important ceremonies and political assemblies.31

of the indigenous population from Cape Cod to Maine. Those who survived did so by blending
tribal lines, altering their culture and language significantly.33 The people of the interior, such as
those living in present day Hooksett, were not affected as dramatically by the plague as coastal

         The seventeenth century saw a vast expanse of war. Passaconaway served as the
sagamon, or leader, of the Pennacooks and created the Pennacook Confederacy in the late
sixteenth century. The native people who occupied the area of Hooksett were now part of a wide
network of tribes from across New Hampshire, Maine, and Massachusetts. Passaconaway
advocated for allyship with the English settlers, believing this to be the best path for survival. He
gave them land and hunting and fishing rights, but England hardened its stance and pushed for
even more control. In 1662, after a petition from Passaconaway, the Pennacooks were granted
lands spanning Manchester, Londonderry, Merrimack, Bedford, and possibly parts of Hooksett,
but it all reverted back to the government upon his death three years later.37
         The relationship between the English and the indigenous people continued to deteriorate
over the next several decades. In 1675, to prevent the Pennacook Confederacy from siding
against them in King Philip’s War, the English burned the Pennacook fort which had been
relocated from Sugar Ball Bluff in Hooksett to Sewall’s Island and destroyed their provision
stores. Continued violence between indigenous people and English settlers across the region was
blamed on the former, and by 1676, the English authorities had killed over 3,000 natives and sold
hundreds more into slavery. This campaign of terror continued until 1685. Survivors fled inland
to escape the slaughter and assimilated with the Pennacooks, further blending the culture and
lifestyles of Hooksett’s indigenous residents.38
         In 1685, a treaty was struck between England and the Pennacooks.39 They taught settlers
how to thrive in the harsh New England climate, such as making maple syrup, farming corn,
crafting snowshoes, and tanning hides. Despite this, there was a cultural pushback against
indigenous people. Settlers pushed them off of their lands, jailed them for crimes they did not
commit, enslaved them, and destroyed their villages to steal their winter provisions.40 The
Pennacook Confederacy was effectively ended in 1689 after sagamon Kancamagus was captured
and his family killed.41
         Evidence of indigenous settlements in Hooksett has been muddled by the encroachment
of industrialization, erasure by English recordkeepers, and the great loss of life brought on by the
disease and warfare of the seventeenth century. Neither the Pennacook Confederacy nor the
Pennacook tribe as a distinct entity remains today. After 1689, many of them scattered, though a

                                    Part Two: The Dundee Mills

        The first mill built at “Annahooksett Falls” was a sawmill constructed of wood in 1787 .
During this time this area was part of the town of Chester. The land where the mills were and
current park is today was passed through many different hands until it was finally acquired by
the Hooksett Manufacturing Company in 1823, just one year after the town was officially
incorporated. The company was founded by John Bell, Samuel Bell, Isaac Hill, and Richard H.
Ayer. They constructed a cotton mill on the site that same year, close to the location of an old
sawmill, to harness the water;s power. It was three stories tall, forty feet wide and eighty feet
long, and run by tub wheels.45 The mill was acquired by the Amoskeag Manufacturing Company
of Manchester in 1835, then purchased by Robert Bailey of Boston in 1865.46
        The main mill building (Mill No. 1) was located on the east bank of the Merrimack River
just below Hooksett Falls. It primarily produced cotton cloth.47 It contained a picker room and
storage areas on the first floor, finishing picker room on the second floor, carding room on the
third floor, and later added a fourth floor with a cloth room.48 Over time, other features were
added to the site. A carpentry shop was added in 1862 and an additional mill building (Mill No.
2) in 1866. The largest expansion was in 1896, when the Amoskeag Manufacturing Company
added a weave shed, offices, boiler house, and engine house. The final addition was that of Store
No. 1 in 1900.49
        The buildings were constructed of several different materials. Most of the older buildings
were made of bricks, which were conveniently produced just north of the mills in the Head
Brickyard, while the main storehouse was constructed of heavy timber and the weave shed and
the tenements located along Merrimack Street and present day Dundee Avenue were built of
wood. There were 29 tenements, all built c. 1890, ranging from one to two and a half stories and
100 to 2,600 square feet. There were also two one-story boarding houses, 100 and 200 square
feet respectively, as well as two agents’ residences; one of them was one story with 324 square
feet, and the other was two stories with 423 square feet. One of the residential lots contained a
one-story barn which was also built around 1890.50 Some of the buildings received electric light
in 1896, which was provided by the Hooksett Electric Light and Power Company right next door
(the site of the current Hooksett Hydro Dam). This company later built a substation just north of
the mill in 1902, which provided 600V DC power.51
        At the time of the closing of the Dundee Mill in 1929, the complex contained a mix of
old and new equipment. The weave room contained relatively new automatic looms.52 The

                           Part Three: Alfred J. Lambert Riverside Park

        Alfred “Fred” J. Lambert was a lifelong advocate for land conservation. He was born in
Lowell, MA on 26 May 1904.68 His parents were Henry J. and Victoria Lambert (née Mathon)69
from Canada.70 His family moved to Hooksett when he was eleven years old and he spent the rest
of his life there. As a young man, he worked as a shipping foreman for Emerson Manufacturing
Co., which occupied the site of the former Dundee Mills.71 At the age of 36, he married Alice O.
Wilson, daughter of Eugene R. and Amelie (sometimes listed as Aurelie) Wilson (née
Paquette).72
        Throughout the course of his life, Fred was incredibly involved in the town and
passionate about protecting the land. He was a New Hampshire Fish and Game commissioner, a
member and later secretary of Pinnacle Fish and Game Club, and a founding member of the
Hooksett Conservation Commission.73 Under his leadership and with the help of a grant from the
Land and Water Conservation Fund, in 1973 the town of Hooksett acquired the land along the
Merrimack River which was the former site of the Dundee Mills. The town combined it with
nearby land it already owned to create a “multi-purpose recreational area” then known as
Riverside Park.74
        Fred Lambert witnessed the multiple iterations of this historic land in his lifetime, from
watching the Dundee Mills in operation as a boy, to becoming a young man working there under
C.S. Emerson Company, experiencing the closure of the mills, the demolition, and then years of
the abandoned property being used as a dumping ground. Fred had a desire to revive the area and
make it something special for the people of Hooksett. During his time on the Hooksett
Conservation Commission, he oversaw the restoration of this scenic riverfront property from a
barren landscape to a flourishing recreation area. He protected the area from future development
and neglect, ensuring it would be available for Hooksett residents to enjoy for generations, and
sold some of the town land to create a state boat launch in partnership with the NH Fish and
Game Department. There was originally a beach and swim area, but with the installation of the
boat launch and vehicle parking, swimming was disallowed due to safety concerns.
        Fred Lambert passed away on 8 June 1980.75 He was survived by his wife, Alice, who
passed away on 14 February 1984. Both of them are buried in Hooksett’s Head Cemetery.76 To
honor his legacy of conservation and service to the town of Hooksett, the park was rededicated in
1982 as A.J. Lambert Riverside Park. There was some minor confusion in 1985 when several
foundation blocks left over from Dundee Mill building No. 1 were removed and the park

Lambert Park History
Pre-European Civilization
         The Merrimack Valley was formed over millions of years of cooling and warming
periods. Glaciers melted into freshwater basins and temperate forests, creating an ideal
environment for human settlement. Human beings first arrived in the Merrimack Valley at least
13,000 years ago, but they were largely nomadic. It was not until the growth of agriculture
around 3,000 years ago that humans began to establish long-term settlements and formed the
tribes with which we are familiar today.
Pre-European Lifestyles
         It is largely accepted that Hooksett was the primary territory of the Pennacook, who
belonged to the Algonquian language family. They referred to the Merrimack River as
N'amaskik, or Namoskeag ("place of many fish"). There were other tribes who lived in Hooksett,
such as the Nashua, Squamscot, and Piscataqua; but these were later merged into the Pennacook
Confederacy.
         Life in Hooksett was relatively cyclical. The fertile land along the Merrimack was used to
sow crops, such as corn, beans, pumpkins, squash, and tobacco. They also gathered foraged in
the rich woods and hunted moose, deer, and caribou. During fishing seasons, they traveled to
Amoskeag Falls in present-day Manchester, where salmon, shad, and lamprey abounded. They
stored provisions at their winter settlement in Pennacook, near present-day Concord.
         They carved ceramic vessels, made clothing from animal skins decorated with beads and
feathers, and crafted jewelry of polished wampum. Fine cooking or crafting tools were made of
shell or bone, while those for more arduous task were made of stone or wood. They used granite
tools, but also materials not local to the region, such as soapstone and jasper. This is evidence of
the complex trade routes throughout New England. Rivers were like roads, filled with heavy
dugouts and slim canoes carrying people and goods. Trade also fed the complex kin networks
stretching across tribes.
The Pennacook and the English
         Passaconaway united the native peoples of present-day Hooksett under the Pennacook
Confederacy. When English settlers first arrived in 1605, they noted that the local name for the
area around the falls was “Annahooksett,” meaning “place of beautiful trees.” Passaconaway saw
an alliance with the English as the best path to survival. This was not to be. Between the ravages
of disease and conflict, the Pennacook population went from about 8,000 people in 1605 to just
1,250 people by 1674. Even so, Passaconaway’s son and heir, Wonalancet, refused to take arms
against the English during King Philip’s War. In 1685, the Pennacooks and the English signed a
formal peace treaty. They taught settlers how to survive the harsh New England winters and
valuable uses of the land, such as making maple syrup, crafting snowshoes, and farming corn.
Despite this, they were treated harshly and displaced from their land. Many joined Algonquian
tribes in Maine and Massachusetts or fled north to Quebec, where their descendants live today.
While they are not a federally recognized tribe, the Pennacook people, history, and traditions
remain in New Hampshire and contribute to our rich cultural heritage.

Lambert Park History
The Mill Era
The Hooksett Manufacturing Co.
         Hooksett Falls has been the site of mill activity since the late 18th century. The Hooksett
Manufacturing Company acquired this land in 1823 and built a cotton mill.
The Mill Buildings
         The main building (Mill No. 1) was on the east bank of the Merrimack River, just below
the falls. It contained picker rooms, carding rooms, and later, a fourth floor with a cloth room.
Throughout the 19th century, the site gained a carpentry shop, Mill No. 2, a weave shed, boiler
house, and engine house. The last addition was Store No. 1 in 1900. It also contained housing for
the mill workers. There were 29 tenements, all built c. 1890 and ranging from 100 to 2,600
square feet. One of the lots contained a one-story barn which housed horses and cows. In 1896,
the neighboring Hooksett Electric Light and Power Company provided 600V DC power to some
of the mill buildings.
Changing Hands
         The Amoskeag Manufacturing Company based in nearby Manchester acquired the mill in
1835, then it was purchased by Robert Bailey of Boston in 1865. By 1880, the mill was
producing 5 million yards of fabric per year. Investors acquired it in 1901 and it was officially
renamed the Dundee Mills Company.
Liquidation
         Business slowed down considerably in the 1920s. The Dundee Mill Co. was formally
liquidated in early 1929 and the buildings and equipment auctioned off. Furniture manufacturer
C.S. Emerson Company (later Emerson Manufacturing Company) acquired Mills No. 1 and 2 as
well as several of the auxiliary buildings. The site survived the flooding of 1936, but business did
not continue in Hooksett for much longer. Emerson purchased the Webster Mill in Suncook in
1943 and moved operations there. They tore down the remaining Dundee buildings and sold the
land.

Lambert Park History
Becoming A Park
Alfred “Fred” J. Lambert
         Born in Lowell, MA in 1904, Alfred “Fred” Lambert was a lifelong advocate for land
conservation. His family moved to Hooksett when he was eleven years old and he lived there for
the rest of his life. Throughout the course of his life, he was incredibly involved in the town and
passionate about protecting it. He was a New Hampshire Fish and Game commissioner, a
member and later secretary of Pinnacle Fish and Game Club, one of the town’s first Water
Commissioners, and a founding member of the Hooksett Conservation Commission.
Lambert Park
         As a young man, Fred Lambert had worked as a foreman for the Emerson Manufacturing
Co. at the site of the former Dundee Mills. After the company ceased operations in Hooksett in
1943, the land became a barren dumping ground. Fred soon made it his mission to convert the
spot into a recreation area for the people of Hooksett to enjoy. He worked together with the
Emerson Manufacturing Company and the Town of Hooksett to have the town purchase the land
and set it aside to be made into a public park. It still contained remnants of old mill buildings,
making it a spot of historical and aesthetic value. Fred oversaw the restoration of this former
industrial site back into a scenic riverside recreation area worthy of its historical name, “place of
beautiful trees.” He passed away in June of 1980, having seen his mission realized. He is buried
in Head’s Cemetery alongside his wife, Alice. In 1982, the park was officially dedicated as A.J.
Lambert Riverside Park to honor his efforts in protecting this beautiful slice of land. The
property was expanded in 1985 and renamed Lambert’s Park.
         Today, the park encompasses 1.7 acres and includes numerous amenities. For many years
it was the site of the town’s Old Home Day celebrations, which were revived by Fred’s brother
Paul Lambert in the 1980s. Memories of those celebrations are preserved in the large grill pits on
the site. An ADA-compliant pavilion was constructed in 2020, Eagle Scout projects installed
picnic tables and a stone patio around the flagpole, and in 2023 horseshoe pits were added.
         From bands of nomadic Paelo-Indians, to the churning wheels of industry, to a peaceful
homage to New Hampshire’s natural beauty, this park tells the story of Hooksett. It is dedicated
to Fred Lambert to honor his legacy and provide a place of gathering and recreation for the
people of Hooksett.

                               FRAUD POLICY

                              Section 1. Introduction

1.1 Financial accountability is a top priority for the Town of Hooksett. The Town's
fraud policy formalizes the expectations of personal honesty and integrity required of
Town officials and employees. The Town of Hooksett is committed to protecting its
revenue, property, information and other assets from any attempt, either by members of
the public, contractors, sub-contractors, agents, intermediaries or its own officials and
employees, to gain by deceit, financial or other benefits at the expense of the Hooksett
taxpayers.

                                Section 2. Purpose

2.1     The purpose of this document is to communicate municipal policy regarding the
deterrence and investigation of suspected misconduct and dishonesty by officials or
employees, and to provide specific instructions regarding appropriate action in case of
suspected violations.

                               Section 3. Definition

3.1    There are three major categories of fraud, Asset Misappropriation, Corruption and
Fraudulent Statements.

       1) Asset Misappropriations - Theft or misuse of an asset .
           • Cash
                 o Fraudulent Disbursements        - Perpetrator causes the Town to
                     disburse funds through some trick or device (e.g. submitting false
                     invoices, time sheets, expense reimbursement schemes, check
                     tampering, etc.), or prevents the timeliness of the disbursement of
                     funds.

                  o   Embezzlement - Perpetrator appropriates monies fraudulently to
                      one's own use, as money or property entrusted in one's care.

                  o   Skimming - Cash is stolen from the Town before it is recorded on
                      the Town's books and records.

                  o   Cash Larceny - Cash is stolen from an organization after it has
                      been recorded on the Town's books and records.


                                        Page 1


                                                                                            Page 44 of 79
                                                                                         Agenda Item #10.5.


          •   Inventory and all other assets.
                  o Misuse - Misuse, misappropriation, misapplication, destruction,
                      removal, or concealment of the Town's inventory or assets for
                      personal use (e. g. Town vehicles, computers, supplies, etc.)

                  o   Larceny - Inventory or other assets are stolen from the Town.

       2) Corruption - Wrongfully use influence in a business transaction in order to
       procure some benefits for themselves or another person, contrary to duty to
       employer or the rights of another.

                  o   Conflicts of Interest - An undisclosed economic or personal
                      interest in a transaction that adversely affects the Town.

                  o   Bribery - The offering, giving, receiving or soliciting of anything
                      of value to influence an official act or a business decision.

                  o   Illegal Gratuities - A party who benefits from an official act or a
                      business decision gives a gift to a person who made the decision.
                      An illegal gratuity does not require proof of intent to influence.

                  o   Economic Extortion - An official or employee demands that a
                      vendor/contractor/etc. pay to influence an official act or a business
                      decision.

       3) Fraudulent Statement - Falsification of a Town record.          Other similar
       irregularities is defined as any activity involving questionable behavior or
       business dealings by member of the public, contractors, vendors, agents or Town
       officials and employees, that put Town revenue, property, information and other
       assets at the risk of waste or abuse.

                            Section 4. Zero Tolerance

4.1      The Town of Hooksett has adopted a zero tolerance policy regarding fraud. No
official or employee of the Town shall remove any Town of Hooksett assets from the
property without proper authorization from the Town Administrator, misuse any Town
assets for ones personal gain, or willfully misappropriate any Town of Hooksett asset.
Any evidence supporting fraud, theft or embezzlement of Town of Hooksett assets and
equipment may be subject to the following personnel actions including but not limited to:
suspension, termination, and restitution.


                Section 5. Reporting of Fraud or Corruption

5.1     Officials and employees shall read and understand this policy. Department Heads
shall be responsible to communicate this policy to their staff.


                                         Page 2


                                                                                              Page 45 of 79
                                                                                          Agenda Item #10.5.


5.2     Any Town of Hooksett official or employee who suspects or is aware of fraud
being committed against the Town by anyone shall immediately report such activity to
the Town Administrator. (If the Town Administrator is the alleged perpetrator the report
shall be directed to the Hooksett Police Chief. If this occurs, the Chief will take the place
of the Administrator in the process described below.)

Upon receiving such a report, the Town Administrator will immediately take the
allegation to the Town's Legal Counsel and the Hooksett Police Prosecutor.            The
Administrator, Counsel and Prosecutor will decide the most prudent way to proceed.
This may include, for example, parallel investigations for personnel or criminal issues or
a decision that the investigation should be completely turned over to the Hooksett Police
Department or the NH State Police.

Once a plan of action has been developed, the Town Council will be apprised of the
situation in a non-public session.

5.3    All investigations will be conducted in confidence insofar as reasonably possible.
The names or names of those communicating information about a fraudulent act or the
name or names of those suspected of a fraudulent act will only be revealed when required
by law in conjunctions with the investigation or legal action.


                            Section 6. False Allegations

6.1    False allegations of suspected fraud with the intent to disrupt or cause harm to
another may be subject to disciplinary action up to and including termination of
employment.

6.2     NH Whistleblowers' Protection Act (RSA 275-E) was established to protect the
rights of officials or employee who report violation of law, or refuse to execute illegal
directives, participate in investigations or hearings. For a full explanation of these rights
the remedies, officials and employees should review this policy which should be
displayed in a prominent location in their place of work.

                                Section 7. Prevention

7.1    Each department will maintain an internal control environment to protect the
department and the Town from loss or other damages as a result of a fraudulent act.


                 Section 8. Corrective Actions and Discipline

8.1 . Appropriate and timely action will be taken against those proven to have
comrmtted fraudulent act. These remedial actions may include, but are not limited to:

                                                                                          Agenda Item #10.5.


   1. Disciplinary action (up to and including immediate termination of employment).
   2. Restitution for all losses, including investigation and legal expenses, to the fullest
      extent of the law.
   3. Institution of civil action to recover losses.
   4. Where the Town of Hooksett elects to take corrective or disciplinary action, it will
      proceed under the procedures in place under the Personnel Plan or under any
      collective bargaining agreement for the respective employment classification.
   5. The Town of Hooksett may take corrective or disciplinary action without awaiting
      the resolution of criminal or civil proceedings arising from fraudulent conduct.

the property and the fact that there are multiple interested parties, a sealed bid process is the best
method for disposing of the property.

The proposed process is as follows:
   1. The Town Council authorizes the sale of 1558 Hooksett Road, Unit C1B through a sealed bid
      process.
   2. The interested parties are notified of the approved sale process and provided with the bid
      requirements.
   3. Because the Town acquired the property by tax deed less than three years ago, 90-day
      notices are sent to the former owner and any mortgagees, as required by RSA 80:89.
   4. The Town waits the required 90-day statutory period.
   5. If the property is not repurchased by the former owner during that period, the Town proceeds
      with advertising the sale and conducting the sealed bid process.

Move forward with the process as outlined above.
