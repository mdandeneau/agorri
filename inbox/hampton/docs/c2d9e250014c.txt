IV.        Appointments
              a. Nicholas Caporale, 2026 Intern Presentation
       -   Notes:
              o N. Caporale presented on his conservation-monitoring internship. His role involved inspecting Town
                  conservation parcels, confirming boundaries where possible, documenting site conditions, and identifying
                  potential encroachments or dumping.
                       Using existing survey pins, granite bounds, stone walls, temporary field markers, and the OnX
                          mapping application, he navigated parcels and recorded observations.


                                                                                                                         1
                        He documented native, introduced, and invasive plant and fungal species encountered during
                         monitoring. Some of Hampton's conservation lands include varied habitats, including forest,
                         freshwater wetland, salt marsh, and open areas.
                      N. Caporale discovered several concerns within conservation parcels during site monitoring.
                         These included yard-waste/trash dumping, temporary structures that were not
                         documented/permitted, scrap piles, and an invisible-fence boundary that extended ~30 ft into the
                         parcel boundary.
                      Yard waste can smother native plants, introduce lawn chemicals and excess nutrients, encourage
                         invasive species, and create fire and pest concerns.
             o Overall, his recommendation to the Commission is to install more conservation-land placards at obvious
                 road frontages and confirmed boundaries to reduce confusion and discourage future dumping. This could
                 be done by future interns.
     -   Discussion:
             o How closely did the OnX parcel boundaries correspond with boundary evidence in the field?
                      In some areas, the boundaries were exactly as mapped. Some mapped boundaries were generally
                         within ~10 feet of field markers.
                      B. Hagan: This was consistent with information received from surveyors and the Town Forest
                         stewardship team. OnX parcel lines are useful for navigation but are derived from tax maps and
                         are not a substitute for a survey.
             o Will the waypoints and field observations remain available for future monitoring?
                      The waypoints were saved to N. Caporale’s OnX account.
                      B. Hagan: The intern data will be compiled through the Town's business account so future interns
                         can locate prior access points, boundary evidence, dumping, water features, and other
                         observations.
             o Will there be follow-up on the invisible fence, the unauthorized structures, and other concerns?
                      B. Hagan will review the intern reports and send letters regarding identified infractions during the
                         fall and winter. She will organize the work to allow manageable follow-up and enforcement.
             o Could cleanups be organized for the more significant dumping areas?
                      B. Hagan: Responsibility should generally remain with the party responsible for the dumping. For
                         older material, the Town may document the site and compare conditions during the next
                         monitoring cycle before pursuing enforcement.
                      It is a good idea for future interns to carry conservation-land placards and install them where a
                         road frontage or documented boundary is clear.
             o A member of the public: Do the concerns about yard-waste dumping also apply to buffer zones?
                      Conservation land does not have a single general buffer designation.
                      Wetland and wetland-buffer regulations apply where those resources occur, but dumping is
                         inappropriate on public conservation land regardless of whether a wetland buffer is present.
             o D. Wrobel thanked N. Caporale for the presentation as it will assist the Commission's stewardship work.

VI.       Old Business
             a. 2027 Warrant Articles
      -   Notes on warrant article regarding the definition of pervious and impervious surfaces:
             o B. Hagan went over Planning Board feedback on the proposed ordinance revisions.
                      The definition of impervious surface and the standards about decks and patios will likely be
                          placed in separate sections of the ordinance.
             o The Commission discussed the range of permeability among decks, patios, and natural vegetation. The
                 Commission also discussed the value of limiting extensive hardscape in the Wetland Conservation
                 District and Groundwater Protection District.
             o Commission members reached consensus to move forward with language treating decks and patios
                 consistently and will propose limiting the area of decks and patios that can be considered pervious in the
                 Wetland Conservation District and Groundwater Protection District to 160 sq ft.
             o No formal vote was taken.
             o B. Hagan will continue refining the language for the warrant article process.

      -   Notes on warrant article regarding the language of Repair or Replacement of an Existing Structure:
             o After meeting with the Planning Board, B. Hagan went over the proposed revisions intended to clarify
                 when a previously permitted structure may be repaired or replaced without a new Town Wetland Permit.

                b. Ice Pond Discussion
        -   NHDES staff did not anticipate that dredging or hydro-raking with herbicide treatment would qualify as a
            restoration project. Either option would likely require full permitting.
        -   Dredging would likely require mitigation which could cost approximately $1 million or more.
        -   Professional and State permitting costs for either major approach could also be substantial, and disturbance
            exceeding 5,000 sq ft could trigger U.S. Army Corps of Engineers review.
        -   NHDES staff also expressed reservations regarding herbicide use.
        -   The Commission discussed the difficulty of permitting work in a freshwater pond, the possible ecological effects,
            and whether a broader sitewide approach could better support a restoration rationale.
        -   B. Hagan will continue gathering information regarding feasibility, permitting, potential compensatory mitigation,
            and warrant-article options.
        -   The Commission has time to continue evaluating the issue before the December warrant deadline.
