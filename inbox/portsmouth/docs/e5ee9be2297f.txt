Communities Freedom Energy serves with municipal energy contracts, Community
Choice Aggregation (CCA) and combined:


Currently CCAs in Salem, Londonderry, Windham, Merrimack and Laconia. Hudson and
Epping are leaving CPCNH to join Freedom Energy. Epping and Hampstead expect to
launch in Spring 2027. Shostak said Freedom Energy never launches a CCA unless the
rate is lower than the utility.

All energy supply comes from third-party suppliers, with Freedom Energy as broker
helps municipalities choose through a competitive bid process. Once the contract is
signed, the supplier bears the risk, but the municipality – not Freedom Energy – is
responsible for the choice. Freedom Energy charges a 10 cents per kWh which is built
into the contracted rate and not charged until launch.

Contracts can run for 6 months or up to 5 years, with a ‘sweet spot’ at 2-3 years. It is
possible to split. Salem contracted a rate through November 2027 before DASI hit, with
that charge going from 0.1 cent to 1.0 cent, so they decided to split the contract to 50%
at 12.676 through November 2030 and the anticipation of beating the rate in January
2027 negotiations. When the second rate is determined the price is averaged across the
full contract. Once contracted the supplier can’t change the rate.

Charette asked -- once the contract is in place with the supplier, what is if the utility rate
is less than the contract and even all of the city’s customers opt out? A. The under-
collection impact would be to the supplier, not the city.

Somssich asked about technical expertise as the city is not as well-versed. A. Howard X
has decades of experience in energy tech and Freedom Energy, because of its large
ISO-direct customers, is considered the expert in ISO New England.

Somssich asked about New England reliance on natural gas and the impact of that and
global situation on the volatility of energy pricing. A. That is hard to predict but
underscores the benefit of locking in a supplier rate.

Freedom Energy:
   • Is the broker administrator rather than the supplier.
   • Assists in timing and strategy, eg a municipality like Hudson exiting CPCNH in
      the middle of a rate period.
   • Handles marketing: customer information including refreshes.
   • Assists with adder fees for renewables or community projects reserves.
   • Creates the supplier RFP and supplier v. utility recommendation based on
      analytics.
   • Looks for futures, opportunities through the life of the contract.
   • The supplier contract locks a price, not the volume. Freedom’s fee doesn’t
      change with volume changes
   • Customer service handled through a dedicated phone line and service center in
      Auburn NH. Opting-out or in is simple: Freedom website, email, phone or through
      the supplier with Freedom Energy sending the drop notification from the supplier
      to the utility.
   • Does not collect reserves so nothing impacted by rate volatility or under-
      collection.


Charette asked if Freedom Energy has select suppliers. A. Yes. 4 that provide CCA
rates. None has ever defaulted.

Chair Tabor asked about green options. A. Freedom suppliers have to comply with the
renewables percentage/REC requirements. Their Director of Renewable Operations
joined a year ago. The premium for renewables is added to the rate. The RECs are from
NY/New England sources.

Charette asked whether Freedom Energy offers CCAs in Massachusetts. A. No.
Colonial Power covers that market. CCAs are a small percentage of Freedom’s
business; their main income is from large ISO-direct commercial customers.

Somssich asked Freedom to define its benefits over CPCNH. A:
  • Rate protection
  • More stability on rates due to contract
  • No risk to resident customers, supplier accepts the risk
  • Fixed contracts are easier to understand/manage
  • Contract is exempt from market volatility
  • Long-term contracts mean long-term stability

Somssich asked if an autonomous larger member such as Portsmouth would be less
beholden to the smaller community members. Portsmouth gets 1 vote even though the
city is providing a large load and funding to help keep CPCNH running.

Chair Tabor asked how often the customer list is refreshed, an issue in Portsmouth with
50% of customers in apartments. Delayed refreshing creates two ‘classes’ of energy
customers such that residents don’t understand why they’re paying more/less than their
neighbors. A. Freedom refreshes with new utility accounts, but not those who have
opted out, and not unless the town’s supplier rate is lower than the utility. A. Freedom
Energy will provide more specific details.

Chair Tabor asked if the ‘sweet spot’ is 2-3 years, but suddenly global factors cause the
rates to drop dramatically and the contracted rate is much higher than the utility default.
A. Freedom informs customers, who can opt-out. Even all of them. Charette commented
that people don’t, either because they’re not aware or they don’t understand the impact.
There is no customer waiting period to go back to the utility.

Charette asked and confirmed that when rates fluctuate, it’s possible to ‘blend and
extend’ by purchasing a contract add-on at the lower rate, which is then averaged with
the current rate.

Chair Tabor asked whether in 3-5 years, Freedom Energy will still be doing CCAs. A.
Since the contract is with the supplier. It continues whether Freedom Energy is around
or not.

Chair Tabor asked whether Freedom Energy is, like CPCNH, active in lobbying the NH
Legislature. A. Yes. CEO is active.

Chair Tabor offered a pro/con summary:
  • Freedom Energy is a longer contract, reached through an RFP process, to get a
       consistently good rate.
  • The rate is stable with the risk on the contractor.

   •   Contract remains even if the utility rate is lower – the customer can opt-out but
       not the city.
   •   The trend remains rising prices for supply and potentially delivery charges as
       well. A. The utility has no incentive as it can’t profit from the supply, instead their
       profits mean reliance on more transmission investment in the aging
       infrastructure, which pushes delivery costs up.

Somssich asked about supply from small nuclear reactors. A. Yes, in the future.

Chair Tabor asked if Shostak and Plante would entertain follow-up questions. A. Yes.

The committee thanked Shostak and Plante for their time and presentation.

After their departure Charette suggested that PEAC schedule one or more briefings
from CPCNH, especially the new procurement and financial directors, to get an update
and comment on the broker model. 2023 – when CPCNH started – is not 2026.

Somssich: with Freedom Energy as the broker, they can structure procurement for the
customer. Portsmouth is subject to CPCNH portfolio management across the
membership.

Chair Tabor: CPCNH offers the lobbying and community power projects that Freedom
does not. The broker model is purely about the money and Freedom Power
understands procurement strategy in this regulatory environment. Freedom Energy, the
broker model, is about a fixed contracted rate, which is where we started with
Eversource as the default utility.

Chair Tabor: The CPCNH member meeting will discuss their goal of a minimum of 60
days of operating reserves (at 9 days at the end of Aug) while comparable entities are
saying a minimum of 120 days. Why not CPCNH?

Charette commented that Freedom Energy has scant history in CCAs and is not
operating in MA where they are burgeoning. PEAC needs to understand CPCNH’s
financial outlook and their perspective on brokers.
