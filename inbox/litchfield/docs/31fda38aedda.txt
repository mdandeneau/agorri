    B.​ Salt Shed Purchase (Tabled from September 28, 2026)
    Building Inspector Memo Regarding Shed
    Salt Shed Proposal

    The Board continued its discussion of the proposed salt shed, tabled at the September 28 meeting. Mr. Mellen
    presented the revised design and discussed the proposed materials, explaining that he had researched options that
    would withstand exposure to road salt while providing an attractive, historically inspired appearance.

    The proposed design included cedar siding, rough-cut boards, PVC trim to help prevent rot, and a metal roof. The
    structure would provide secure storage for road salt while allowing the area to serve a secondary purpose, including
    community fundraising activities during inclement weather. Members discussed the importance of selecting durable
    materials that could withstand salt exposure and the potential need for interior protective sheeting or a sacrificial wall
    to protect the framing and metal components from corrosion.

    Board members acknowledged that the project had been under consideration for several years. They discussed the
    costs associated with constructing the shed, noting that the proposed expense was approximately $11,000. Some
    members questioned whether the cost was justified for a structure intended primarily to store salt. In contrast, others
    emphasized the importance of protecting the salt supply, improving the facility's appearance, and providing a safer
    and more functional storage area.

    The Board discussed the limitations of leaving salt uncovered or placing it behind a simple concrete-block barrier.
    Members explained that the original concern involved preventing people from accessing the rear of the facility and
    potentially falling, as well as finding a suitable location for salt storage that would not interfere with vehicle traffic or
    other activities. The proposed location was considered practical because it provided adequate working space and
    allowed vehicles to access the shed without obstructing the roadway.

    Members also discussed constructing the shed using salvaged materials or a less expensive design. However, they
    noted that obtaining a contractor and suitable materials at a lower cost could be difficult given current construction
    prices. The proposed design was intended to provide a durable, functional structure that would improve the facility's
    appearance and offer additional community use.

    The Board discussed the need to evaluate the shed's operation after construction, including the placement of the salt
    containers and how easily vehicles can enter and leave the facility. Members agreed that adjustments may be needed
    over time to ensure the structure functions as intended.

    The Board discussed the proposed acquisition of an 8-foot-by-12-foot wood bin-style shed and the funding source.
    Members discussed whether funds could be allocated from an existing trust fund established for this type of purpose.
    The Board also raised concerns about the shed's interior safety, particularly the potential for exposed nails or other
    hazards if the interior walls were not fully finished. Members noted that additional interior wall coverings might be
    necessary to ensure the space would be safe for children and other occupants if used for multiple purposes.

    Mr. Mellen acknowledged that the project had been under consideration for a long time and that considerable effort
    had gone into its development. Although the members expressed reservations, they indicated support for moving
    forward with the project while monitoring whether additional funding would be necessary. The Board discussed
    contacting Reeds Ferry Sheds to ask for a discounted price if the company's name was placed on the shed. (e.g., Shed
    provided by Reeds Ferry Sheds).

    Mr. Leary mentioned a previous request for a discounted gazebo for Campbell for the field dedication. Reeds Ferry
    Sheds ultimately donated the gazebo, and the members noted the company's willingness to assist with community
    projects.

    D.​ Review of Revenue – 2026 and 2027
    MS-434-R
    Estimated revenues 2027 - 2026 Revised Revenues
    Estimated revenues 2027 - 9 months

    Ms. Kleiner reviewed the revised revenue estimates following the annual revenue review with the New Hampshire
    Department of Revenue Administration (DRA). The revised MS-434R reflects updated revenue figures that affect the
    tax cap for the proposed 2027 budget.

    Meals and Rooms Tax revenue had been budgeted at $810,000 but was received at $912,571, exceeding the estimate.
    The Town also received $213,000 in Highway Block Grant revenue, compared with the $200,000 budgeted. These
    increases required adjustments to the revenue estimates for the current fiscal year and the following budget year.

    The increased revenues for 2026 reduced the Town’s tax effort for the current year and consequently reduced the
    available capacity under the 5% tax cap for 2027. The amount available under the cap decreased from approximately
    $32,074 to $24,845. Ms. Kleiner noted that the increased revenues, combined with the previously reported $59
    million increase in assessed valuation, should help reduce the 2026 tax rate for taxpayers.

    Ms. Kleiner reported that the Town submitted the revised revenue documentation to the State. The Town was

     E.​ Letter to DOT – 48 Old Derry Road
     20261001 Letter - Response to DOT State Owned Properties

    The Board discussed a proposed letter to the New Hampshire Department of Transportation (DOT) requesting a
    30-day extension to evaluate state-owned properties for potential municipal use. At its September 28 meeting, the
    Board had requested that a letter be drafted seeking additional time to evaluate the properties.

    Ms. Kleiner reported that DOT contacted the Town regarding the property at 48 Old Derry Road. DOT questioned
    whether the property served a necessary municipal purpose and indicated that the State had incurred substantial
    costs maintaining and retaining it, including costs associated with an eviction. If the Town did not intend to use the
    property, DOT wished to dispose of it through a sale or other means.

    The Board discussed whether requesting a 30-day extension would meaningfully advance the Town's efforts to acquire
    the properties. Members noted that the Town could not make a final decision until the March town meeting, when
    voters could consider the necessary authorizations and funding. Members also expressed concern about competing
    expenditures, including anticipated school-related requests, and the uncertainty surrounding the properties' value,
    acquisition costs, and potential uses.

    The Board acknowledged that pursuing an acquisition would require several preliminary steps, including establishing
    the appropriate municipal economic development organization, preparing warrant articles, determining the
    properties' value, and identifying a funding mechanism. Ms. Kleiner noted that the Town might need to pursue a bond,
    which would require additional legal and financial planning. The Town would need to consult its bonding counsel and
    engage the Mitchell Group to help prepare the necessary warrant articles. These steps would require time,
    coordination, and funding that the Town had not yet secured.

    The Board also discussed whether the Conservation Commission might be interested in acquiring some of the
    state-owned land, particularly property near the river or land that could be preserved for agricultural use. However,
    members indicated that they were unaware of any active interest or commitment from the Commission.

    Members clarified the distinction between the property at 48 Old Derry Road and the other properties under
    consideration, including parcels along Route 102. They noted that selling certain parcels along Route 102 could
    restrict access to approximately 110 acres of land. However, the Board generally agreed that the property at 48 Old
    Derry Road fell outside the scope of the broader property acquisition effort currently under review.

    The Board discussed modifying the proposed letter to remove the 48 Old Derry Road property while maintaining the
    request for a 30-day extension for the remaining properties. Ms. Kleiner acknowledged that DOT had not committed to
    granting additional time and that a 30-day extension would not resolve the longer timeline associated with obtaining
    voter approval and securing funding.

Motion:​         (Selectman J. Brunelle / Selectman R. Leary) Moved to modify the letter to the State by removing the
                 48 Old Derry Road property and formally relinquishing the Town's interest in that property, while
                 continuing to request a 30-day extension for the remaining properties.
Vote: ​ ​        (4-0-0) The motion carried.

    Ms. Kleiner stated that the revised letter would be circulated to Board members for approval before being sent to DOT.

    Conflict of Interest
    Conflict of Interest (PDF) 2001

The Board reviewed concerns about the Town's conflict-of-interest policy, specifically the provision addressing gifts
and favors employees receive from members of the public, vendors, or suppliers. The discussion focused on whether
the existing policy adequately addresses customary gestures of appreciation, such as food, treats, promotional items,
and other items of limited value provided by residents, local businesses, and community members.

Mr. Brunelle discussed a recent incident involving a promotional item distributed to certain individuals. The Board
considered whether the item complied with the existing policy and whether the policy required clarification to
distinguish between nominal gifts and items of significant monetary value. Members noted that employees have
historically received food, refreshments, and other tokens of appreciation without incident.

Mr. Leary said the discussion appeared to stem from personal workplace disagreements rather than a legitimate
conflict of interest. He cited examples of the Selectmen providing meals to Police and Fire Department personnel and
individuals purchasing food for employees in various departments. He emphasized that these gestures had not
previously raised concerns and questioned why similar acts of appreciation should be treated differently.

The Board acknowledged the concerns and emphasized the importance of maintaining a clear and consistently
applied policy. The members discussed the distinction between a gift intended to express gratitude and one that could
influence an employee’s official actions. They agreed that gifts of substantial monetary value or those offered with an
expectation of favorable treatment would raise legitimate concerns.

Ms. Kleiner provided an example involving flowers delivered to Town Hall by friends following a personal matter. She
questioned whether such a gesture should be considered a prohibited gift or a conflict of interest. The Board discussed
the importance of distinguishing personal gifts from those that could compromise an employee’s impartiality.
Members also noted that the recent incident involved a community volunteer rather than a Town vendor or supplier
and that similar items had been distributed to other employees.

The Board reviewed the existing policy language, which prohibits employees from receiving gifts or favors from
members of the public, Town vendors, or suppliers, except for incidental promotional items or items of limited value
that can be shared with other employees. Members discussed establishing a specific monetary threshold, with $25
suggested as a possible limit consistent with the Internal Revenue Service threshold referenced during the discussion.
However, the Board did not adopt a new monetary limit.

After discussion, the Board generally agreed that the existing policy adequately addressed the matter and that the item
in question could be considered of minimal value rather than a conflict of interest. Members emphasized that the
policy should not prevent reasonable gestures of appreciation, such as providing refreshments to employees, while
still protecting the Town against actual or perceived conflicts of interest.

The Board also discussed differences between the conflict-of-interest language in the personnel manual and a policy
associated with a 2011 warrant article approved by voters. Members noted that the two provisions appeared to
address similar concerns, although the warrant article policy was more closely aligned with a code of ethics. The
discussion highlighted the importance of ensuring that employees understand the applicable requirements and that
concerns are addressed through appropriate management channels.

Ms. Kleiner stated that the matter had not been brought to her attention before it reached the Board and that she had
not been allowed to explain the circumstances or identify others who had received similar items. She requested that
future concerns be discussed through the management team, when appropriate, so employees can provide context and
understand differing perspectives.

The Board concluded that the matter did not constitute a conflict of interest under its interpretation of the existing
policy. The appropriate staff member confirmed that she would follow up on the matter. No formal amendment to the
policy was adopted.

H.​ Discussion – TRC/Planning

Litchfield Advisory TRC Administrative Procedure
Technical Review - Operational Flowchart modified 06.30.2025

The Board reviewed the proposed administrative procedures and operational flow chart for the Technical Review
Committee (TRC). Ms. Kleiner explained that many municipalities use a TRC consisting of representatives from
departments that may be affected by development proposals or planning and zoning applications. The Committee's
purpose is to provide the Planning Board and Zoning Board with departmental feedback before they act on a proposal,
allowing potential concerns to be identified and addressed early in the review process.

The first document outlined the standard administrative procedures, while the second presented the operational flow
chart. The Department Heads and Planning Board had reviewed the proposed process and expressed support for the
approach. Ms. Kleiner identified the Planning Assistant and Building Inspector as the two key personnel responsible
for ensuring that applications reach the TRC promptly and that the review process proceeds efficiently.

Ms. Kleiner explained that she had initially assisted with the process because of prior experience in another
municipality. However, coordinating TRC meetings should not be the Town Administrator's responsibility. Instead, the
Town Administrator should receive relevant information and updates from the Committee rather than schedule or
routinely attend its meetings.

The discussion highlighted concerns about incomplete communication and inconsistent property addressing. Mr.
Brunelle stated that in one instance involving a solar project, the Board had not received a letter from the Chief, and
members were unaware that the letter existed. In another instance, a property on Charles Bancroft Highway had
conflicting addresses in the 911 system, the assessing database, and the physical road signage. The discussion
emphasized that the Fire Department should establish physical addresses and forward them to 911 to ensure
consistency among municipal records and emergency response systems.

These examples demonstrated the need for a formalized review process with clear responsibilities, documentation,
and confirmation that all relevant departments receive and review project information. Mr. Brunelle proposed
assigning the Building Inspector responsibility for coordinating TRC meetings, ensuring regular participation by the
appropriate departments, and confirming that the required reviews and documentation are completed. The Board
discussed whether this additional responsibility would be appropriate given the Building Inspector's existing
workload.

Ms. Kleiner reported that Building Inspector Ken Daigle had recently earned his fourth of five certifications,
demonstrating significant professional progress. The Board recognized his qualifications and ability to work
effectively with the Planning Assistant. Members also noted that any future increase in responsibilities could warrant a
review of his compensation.

The Board discussed the importance of establishing a reliable process as development activity increases in Litchfield.
The proposed procedure should also account for the arrival of a new Town Engineer and establish coordination among
the Building Inspector, Planning Assistant, and Town Engineer. The Town must ultimately provide accurate, timely
information to the Nashua Regional Planning Commission (NRPC) to update the Town's tax maps. Members cited
previous discrepancies in the tax maps and stressed the importance of preventing further errors through consistent
documentation and communication.

The Board agreed that the Building Inspector should review the proposed documents, suggest revisions, and work
with the Planning Assistant and other key stakeholders to develop an effective process. The revised procedures will

    and weekends. Ms. Kleiner believed the provider was completing the tasks specified under the existing agreement but
    acknowledged that the current cleaning schedule could leave gaps between service visits. The buildings remained in
    use throughout the week, and some employees had become accustomed to a previous arrangement in which an
    employee performed various duties in addition to cleaning.

    The Board discussed concerns about restroom supplies, trash removal, and cleaning frequency. Members noted that
    employees could address some routine needs, such as replenishing paper products and emptying wastebaskets,
    between scheduled cleaning visits. However, the Board recognized that the Town needed to establish clear
    expectations and determine whether additional cleaning services were necessary to maintain a safe and sanitary
    workplace.

    Ms. Kleiner noted that the proposed scope included deep cleaning, which could substantially increase costs. As an
    example, she cited a previous deep-cleaning service at the cable building that cost $400, despite the building’s
    relatively small size. Members acknowledged that deep cleaning larger facilities, including Town Hall, which has high
    ceilings and multiple restrooms, could be considerably more expensive.
