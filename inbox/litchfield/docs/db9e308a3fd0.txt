   D.​ Adoption of Bylaws
       Litchfield_CIP_Bylaws

       The Committee reviewed proposed changes to its bylaws, particularly provisions governing the Capital Improvement
       Program (CIP) Committee and the appointment of members. Town Administrator Kim Kleiner discussed whether the
       Board of Selectmen should review or approve the CIP bylaws.

       2014
       Article 20: To see if the Town will authorize the Board of Selectmen to appoint a Capital Improvement Program
       Committee pursuant to RSA 674:5, which shall include at least one member of the Planning Board and may include, but
       not be limited to, other members of the Planning Board, the Budget Committee, or the Board of Selectmen, to prepare and
       amend a recommended program of municipal capital improvement projects projected over a period of at least 6 years.

       Mr. Parker reviewed the warrant article authorizing the Town to establish a Capital Improvement Program Committee
       pursuant to RSA 674:5. The warrant article provides that the committee shall include at least one Planning Board
       member and may include members of the Planning Board, Budget Committee, Board of Selectmen, and other
       representatives to prepare and amend recommendations for municipal capital improvement projects over the next six
       years.

       Mr. Plansky noted conflicting versions of the Committee membership adopted on September 7, 2023. One version
       identifies five members representing the Planning Board, Budget Committee, Board of Selectmen, and two citizens,
       while another identifies Planning Board members, two members of the public, a Selectmen’s representative, and a
       School Board member. The Committee agreed that the bylaws should clarify that representatives are selected by their
       respective boards.

       Ms. Kleiner discussed voting and non-voting membership. The Planning Board and School Board representatives are
       voting members. The Budget Committee representative is non-voting, consistent with the Budget Committee’s
       decision.

       The Town Administrator, School Superintendent, Business Administrator, and NRPC Executive Director are also
       non-voting members. These provisions will be addressed in Sections 2.1 and 2.2.

       Members confirmed that citizen representatives serve staggered three-year terms, while the Selectmen’s ex officio
       representative serves a one-year term. The Board of Selectmen fills vacancies. There is no stated limitation on
       consecutive service.

       The Committee also discussed the timing of the CIP process. Warrant articles must be submitted to the Board of
       Selectmen by the first meeting in December for consideration by the Budget Committee. Members expressed concern
       that waiting until December to submit the final CIP could limit the Board of Selectmen’s ability to incorporate CIP
       recommendations into warrant articles. The Committee agreed that it should review the timeline to ensure the CIP
       process does not interfere with other boards’ authority to develop and approve their own warrant articles.

       The Committee will review the bylaw revisions again at the next meeting. Mr. Minkarah will mark up the proposed
       changes and circulate them for review. Sections 1.1, 1.2, and 1.3 were considered acceptable, with additional revisions
       needed to Sections 2.1 and 2.2. The updated document dated August 6, 2026, located in the meeting portal, will be
       used for the review.

    Library Capital Improvement Projects
    Mr. Parked mentioned the library’s CIP projects and the $50,000 threshold. Members noted that the library may
    struggle to reach the threshold because many proposed projects are relatively small. The Committee discussed
    whether multiple projects could be consolidated to meet the threshold but noted that combining projects could make
    individual items less visible to voters.

    Mr. Bourque asked whether smaller projects belong in the CIP or should be presented as individual warrant articles or
    incorporated into the operating budget. The Committee noted that a CIP generally focuses on larger capital
    expenditures requiring long-term planning.

    Mr. Parker recalled that the library had previously considered a major building project and that the impact fee study
    still identifies the library.

    The Committee discussed potentially bundling smaller Library improvements. Mr. Plansky noted that individual
    warrant articles, including an example of $15,000, may be more likely to be approved than larger bundled requests,
    while bundling could also result in individual projects being rejected as part of a larger package.

    The Committee learned that the Library Board of Trustees determines which warrant articles it wishes to submit. The
    Board of Selectmen does not have authority to prevent a library warrant article from being placed on the warrant,
    although it may indicate whether it supports or does not support the article. The library had already submitted its
    warrant articles.

    Members confirmed that the library’s submitted warrant articles did not include the projects currently listed on the
    CIP. The submitted requests included items such as carpeting, tile, insulation, and book fixtures. Members discussed
    whether placeholder amounts were used for certain projects, including a possible chimney project, although the
    library did not specifically discuss the chimney.

    The Committee discussed the possibility of projects being overlooked between the CIP and warrant article processes.
    Ms. Kleiner concluded that projects would remain documented and could be addressed through the appropriate
    warrant article regardless of whether they originated in the CIP. The Committee noted that CIP projects may have
    multiple potential funding sources, including impact fees and warrant articles.


E.​ 2026 Projects Received
    a.​ Conservation Commission
    2027 CIP Project Submission - Conservation Commission

    George Lavash, Chair of the Conservation Commission, presented the Commission’s $2 million request over six
    years for the Capital Improvement Plan (CIP). He explained that the request is a placeholder for potential future land
    purchases. The Conservation Commission currently has approximately $1.1 million in the Conservation Land Fund,
    which receives revenue from land-use taxes when property is removed from current use. The Commission previously
    used a portion of the fund, along with $600,000 in grants and $400,000 from the Land Fund, to purchase the
    Durocher property. No specific property is currently under consideration.

    Mr. Lavash explained that any future purchase would require a warrant article and voter approval. The Commission
    has a public Conservation Land Preservation Strategy outlining its property-selection criteria, decision-making
    process, and preservation goals, including protecting water resources, providing passive recreation, preserving rural
    character, and acquiring property adjacent to existing conservation lands.


    The Committee discussed whether the $2 million placeholder over six years should remain in the CIP. Members
    noted that the request represents a significant potential annual expenditure, approximately $333,333 per year, but
    also recognized that property costs continue to increase. The Conservation Commission stated that it is not currently
    seeking a warrant article to fund the request but wants to retain the item on the CIP so the Town can respond if an
    appropriate property becomes available.

    The Committee also discussed the Conservation Land Fund, noting that the Town voted by warrant article to place
    80% of current-use revenue into the fund. The percentage varies among municipalities and may be directed to general
    revenue in other communities. The Committee agreed to leave the item on the CIP.

    b.​ Fire Department
    2027 CIP Project Submissions (4 submissions)

    Fire Chief Doug Nicoll reported that the Fire Department is not requesting funding this year, despite having several
    items on the CIP. Given the Town’s increasing costs, including significant increases in the Ambulance and Dispatch
    contracts, the Department decided not to seek funding at this time but requested that its future needs remain on the
    CIP.

    Item One
    Chief Nicoll identified the air cascade system as the Department’s highest priority. The existing system was purchased
    in 2002 for approximately $53,000 through a grant and was relocated to the current fire station in 2018. The system
    is increasingly difficult to maintain because parts are unavailable, and it has developed leaks. The system uses a
    seven-stage filtration process to provide breathing air and operates with mineral oil. The Department has had three
    companies attempt repairs, but parts are no longer available.

    The Department has 26 air packs and 59 bottles, which must be periodically refilled because they lose pressure over
    time. The system can operate at pressures up to 6,000 psi. Although current quality testing has found no safety
    problems, Chief Nicoll stated that the system is nearing the end of its useful life. The Department currently maintains
    the system rather than relying on other departments.

    Chief Nicoll stated that the Department is considering a mobile cascade unit costing approximately $85,000 to
    $90,000. A mobile unit could potentially qualify for a grant because it could be made available to surrounding
    communities through mutual aid. However, the Town would still need to provide matching funds. The Department also
    considered a permanently installed replacement, which would cost more and require the Town to fund the entire
    purchase. The existing system is approximately 24 years old.

    Mr. Bourque asked whether neighboring municipalities could share the cost of a mobile unit. Chief Nicoll explained
    that grant programs may support equipment intended for mutual aid, citing the Town’s forestry truck and
    Manchester’s communications truck as examples. A mobile cascade unit could similarly serve multiple municipalities.

    Item Two
    Chief Nicoll then discussed the Department’s air packs and bottles, which were also purchased in 2002. Air packs are
    subject to annual flow testing, while the bottles require hydrostatic testing every three years. The carbon-fiber bottles
    operate at up to 4,500 psi and are rated for 15 years. The Department’s bottles are approximately nine years old and
    remain in service because they continue to pass required testing. Replacement of the air packs and bottles is estimated
    at approximately $350,000 to $375,000.

    Chief Nicoll explained that replacing the equipment incrementally is not practical because firefighters need identical
    equipment for safety and consistency, particularly when operating in dark and hazardous conditions. Ms. Kleiner
    mentioned using the Town’s capital reserve for future equipment purchases. She emphasized that maintaining
    firefighter equipment ultimately protects personnel.

    Chief Nicoll discussed the evolving fire-service equipment standards. He noted that fire gear that was previously


    designed for temperatures of 300 degrees is now designed for 600 degrees, while flashover conditions can exceed
    1,000 degrees. The air packs are an integral part of the protective system. The Department also maintains a 10-year
    replacement plan for its protective gear.

    Item Three
    Chief Nicoll reported that its trucks are currently in relatively good condition, although they are aging. In the fire
    service, frontline fire trucks typically have a service life of approximately 10 to 15 years because of their operating
    hours and use.

    Chief Nicoll reviewed the Fire Department’s vehicle replacement needs. The Town has not purchased a new fire truck
    since 2003 (Engine One). The two most recent vehicles were obtained through outside funding: the current SUD mini
    pumper through a state grant and the previous truck through COVID funding. The COVID-funded truck cost $656,000;
    replacing the same truck today would cost more than $1 million.

    The Department is considering replacement of Engine Four, a 2001 KME, which remains in good condition but
    required approximately $20,000 in repairs this year. The truck was out of service for more than a month while repairs
    were completed. Because older vehicles cannot always accommodate newer computerized technology, repairs can
    become increasingly difficult and costly. The Department recently replaced the pump-control computer after the
    previous unit failed.

    The Department also operates a 1991 tanker, its oldest truck, which remains in excellent condition and is frequently
    used for mutual aid. Chief Nicoll intends to continue operating the tanker until it is no longer viable. When Engine Four
    is eventually replaced, he proposed combining the functions of Engine Four and the tanker by purchasing a quint and
    retiring both vehicles. A quint is a fire engine equipped with a ladder, tank, and pump.

    Chief Nicoll explained that the Department increasingly needs ladder capability because newer homes and chimneys
    are too high to reach with existing ground ladders. A quint would provide additional capability while reducing the
    Department’s reliance on neighboring communities for ladder trucks. He noted that a quint would cost approximately
    $200,000 more than a regular engine. The ladder would not replace a larger ladder truck, which typically has a 75- to
    100-foot reach, but it would provide useful additional capability for the Town.

    Accessibility is also a concern in developments such as Moose Hollow and Bear Run, where long, steep driveways
    require additional reach. The proposed approach would remove one vehicle from service, eliminate its maintenance
    costs, and provide ladder capability through the replacement vehicle.

    Item Four
    Chief Nicoll also reviewed the Department’s remaining engines. Engine One, the second truck due to be replaced, is
    approximately 22 years old and could be retained as a reserve vehicle. Under NFPA standards, a frontline engine is
    generally used for up to 15 years before becoming a reserve vehicle. The Department relies on three engines to
    maintain the required water flow for the types of buildings in Town. The oldest engine is used as a reserve and for
    mutual aid so that the Department can retain its newer apparatus for local emergencies.

    Fire Department – Air Compressor and Air Packs
    The Committee discussed replacement of the cascade system and compressor and whether the compressor and
    breathing apparatus could be replaced separately. Chief Nicoll confirmed that they could be replaced independently.

    When asked which item was the higher priority, Chief Nicoll identified the firefighter air packs as the number one

    c.​ Police Department

    2027 CIP Project Submission - Police Cruisers
    2027 CIP Project Submission - Police Station

    Police Chief Dan Jones reviewed the Department’s two urgent capital requests, beginning with a police cruiser. The
    Department replaced three vehicles last year, which removed several problematic Tahoes from frontline service and
    allowed some vehicles to be moved into detail use.

    Item One – Cruisers
    Two Tahoes remain in service, one with approximately 100,000 miles and the other with mileage in the 40,000-mile
    range. Chief Jones supported the concept discussed by the Board of Selectmen of establishing a separate maintenance
    reserve to reduce annual budget pressure while maintaining funds for vehicle repairs.

    Chief Jones noted, however, that major mechanical problems can keep the Tahoes out of service for extended periods.
    The Tahoe with approximately 100,000 miles was recently out of service for one to one and a half weeks because of
    multiple issues. There have been occasions when all available vehicles were assigned to detail work, leaving
    insufficient vehicles for emergency response. The Department has also experienced a vehicle failing while responding
    to an emergency call.

    The Department outfitted its detail vehicles to function like frontline vehicles so equipment can be transferred quickly
    when needed. Chief Jones said the 100,000-mile Tahoe could reach about 124,000 to 125,000 miles within two years
    and potentially 150,000 miles by the time it is replaced. That vehicle previously required an engine rebuild at
    approximately 47,000 to 48,000 miles, and the underlying reliability concerns remain.

    Chief Jones stated that his concern is not simply the cost of repairs but the loss of vehicles for extended periods and
    the Department’s ability to maintain adequate emergency coverage. He noted that multiple times last year the
    Department had more personnel than available vehicles, and that vehicle downtime averaged about seven to eight
    weeks for frontline vehicles.

    He acknowledged the Town’s difficult budget year and said he had not received clear direction from the Board of
    Selectmen on whether the cruiser request should be placed on the warrant. If the request does not proceed, the
    Department will manage with its existing fleet. He noted that if the Department does not obtain a replacement this
    year, it may need to replace multiple vehicles the following year. The other Tahoe, with approximately 40,000 miles,
    provides some additional time before replacement becomes necessary.

    The Committee discussed the possibility of returning to a one-make vehicle fleet and purchasing vehicles in batches.
    Chief Jones indicated that this would be preferable if it could be incorporated into the budget and kept on schedule.

    Item Two – Building
    Chief Jones next discussed the need for a new police building. He recommended waiting until the School District
    determines its future facility needs before pursuing a new police facility. He cited both the cost and the likelihood that
    voters would be reluctant to approve a police building shortly after voting on school-related capital expenditures.

    Chief Jones suggested that if the school process results in an available facility or other alternative, the Town could
    consider whether it would meet the Police Department’s needs. He did not believe it would be productive to spend
    significant resources studying a new building before the school situation is resolved.

        The Committee discussed whether an addition or annex to the existing police building could provide an interim
        solution. Chief Jones stated that a previous study determined that modifying the existing building would cost more
        than constructing a new facility because the current structure was not designed to accommodate the Department’s
        needs. The study also identified significant safety and procedural deficiencies. Addressing those deficiencies would
        likely cost more than constructing a properly designed facility.


        Chief Jones stated that he would rather wait five or six years and develop a facility designed to meet the Department’s
        long-term needs than construct a temporary solution that could become inadequate within a few years. He
        emphasized the goal of developing a facility that could serve the Department for 30, 40, or 50 years.
