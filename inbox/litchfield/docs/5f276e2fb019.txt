A. GMS Project Presentation
Superintendent Brian O’Connell and Chair Andrew Cutter presented the proposed Griffin Memorial School (GMS)
renovation, reviewing the facility’s condition, proposed improvements, estimated costs, financing options, and anticipated
timeline.

Project Background and Existing Conditions
Mr. Cutter explained that voters rejected a $44 million warrant article for a new elementary school in March 2024. The
School District subsequently evaluated alternatives through a Facilities Improvement Committee (FIC), which
recommended renovating GMS rather than constructing a new school. The School Board authorized the District to work
with Harriman Construction and Harvey Construction, the construction manager, to develop plans and cost estimates.
GMS serves approximately 410 students and is the District’s largest school. The building encompasses approximately
62,000 square feet, including portable classrooms, and 55,000 square feet without them. Its sections date from the 1930s
through the 1970s, with additions completed in the early 1980s. Improvements have been limited since that time.

Building Deficiencies and Proposed Renovation
Mr. Cutter identified significant deficiencies in the first- and second-grade wing, including water infiltration, inadequate
insulation, and a courtyard design exposing classrooms on four sides. Correcting these issues alone would cost between $7
million and $8 million, including necessary heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC), plumbing, and electrical
improvements.

The proposal would demolish this wing and construct a new two-story addition where the portable classrooms currently
stand. The approximately 16,000- to 17,000-square-foot addition would accommodate grades one through four and allow
pre-kindergarten and kindergarten students to move into the main building.

The project would also provide an access road around the building to improve emergency access, increase student drop-off
capacity, reduce morning congestion and vehicle backups onto Route 3A, and accommodate a dedicated bus drop-off lane
and expanded parking.

The original 1930s building, which has a historical marker, would remain as a separate structure without renovation under
the current proposal. It would continue to serve primarily as storage for tables, desks, and other items.

Building Design, Security, and Site Considerations

The gymnasium and cafeteria would remain largely unchanged, although HVAC and lighting upgrades were planned.

Kindergarten and pre-kindergarten classrooms would be located along the main corridor, while the library and Special
Education areas would remain largely unchanged. The administrative offices would be relocated to the area immediately
left of the gymnasium, improving visibility of the parking area, entrances, and other areas of the building.

The two-story addition would include an elevator. The existing baseball and softball fields would remain unaffected, but
the playground would need to be relocated. The current proposal would not affect the existing septic system.

Mr. Perry asked whether fencing would separate the playground from the proposed access road. Mr. Cutter stated that
fencing would need to be considered during design.

Project Timeline and Construction Phasing
The District planned to present the project to voters in March 2027. If approved, construction would begin in 2028 and
take approximately two to two and a half years, with completion anticipated around 2030 or 2031.

Mr. O’Connell explained that construction would proceed in phases to minimize disruption. The team would complete as
much work as possible during summer, particularly in areas occupied by portable classrooms. Students and staff would
remain in existing rooms as long as practicable and move into completed areas as construction progressed. Temporary or
swing spaces would accommodate relocations.

Some unified arts programs might temporarily share rooms or operate from mobile carts. The District would coordinate
available space across its facilities. Administrative offices would be among the last areas relocated because their proposed
location currently contains classrooms that could remain in use during construction. The approach was intended to
minimize classroom moves and educational disruption.

Project Cost and Proposed Bond Financing
Mr. Cutter reported an estimated construction cost of $31 million and a total project budget of approximately $38
million, including soft costs, contingencies, and allowances for unforeseen expenses. The total could decrease slightly as
discussions with the construction managers continued.

The Board of Selectmen had not finalized the financing structure. The School Board was considering level-debt financing
over 25 or 29 years, which would maintain consistent annual payments and simplify budgeting and communication with
taxpayers.

The 29-year option would carry an interest rate approximately 0.25 percentage points higher and incur approximately $6
million more in interest over the bond’s life than the 25-year option. However, the longer term would reduce the initial
annual tax impact by spreading costs over a longer period.

The presenters noted that the financing approach differed from the series of bonds proposed for the 2024 new-school
project, which had confused taxpayers about the financing structure and tax impact. The current proposal used a simpler
structure.

Estimated Taxpayer Impact
Using Zillow data, the presenters used $618,000 as the town’s median home value for illustration. For a home valued at
$618,000, the estimated first-year tax increase would be $297, reflecting the bond’s anticipated mid-year start.

Beginning in the second year, the estimated annual increase would be $458 under the 25-year option or $414 under the
29-year option. Actual impacts would vary by property value. Mr. Cutter clarified that these figures represented the annual
increase, not an additional increase imposed each year; the illustrated annual amount would remain consistent throughout
the applicable repayment period.

The bond schedules were distributed to Select Board members for review and feedback. The next School Board meeting,
scheduled for November 4, would focus on the GMS project, with representatives from Harriman, the architect, and
Harvey, the construction manager, expected to attend. The School Board was expected to make decisions regarding the
project and bond schedules.

Discussion of Project Costs and Alternatives
Ms. Plansky expressed concern about the proposed $38 million cost and discussed alternatives previously considered,
including consolidating seventh- and eighth-grade students with the high school and modifying the existing middle and
elementary schools. She acknowledged potential cost differences but raised concerns about placing younger students in
the high school environment.

Mr. Cutter acknowledged the expense and noted that the previously proposed new school had been estimated at $44
million. The Select Board cautioned that delays could increase construction costs, with estimates suggesting annual
increases of approximately 3.5%.

Mr. O’Connell stated that the estimates reflected current construction market conditions and assessments by the architect
and construction manager. The FIC had evaluated consolidation and other alternatives but found that combining schools
would not necessarily reduce costs and could prove more expensive. He emphasized that significant construction
expenditures would eventually be necessary regardless of the approach selected.

The District was also managing its operating budget to limit additional taxpayer pressure. Although it did not anticipate a
completely flat budget, it expected the following year’s increase to remain relatively modest.

Health Insurance Savings and Budget Management
Mr. Cutter reported that, following an unexpected SchoolCare assessment affecting the District and other school districts
statewide, the School Board evaluated alternative insurance arrangements. The District moved from SchoolCare to a
captive insurance model with another provider, effective July 2026. He expected the change to generate meaningful
taxpayer savings while maintaining good employee health insurance coverage.

Bond Interest Rates and Repayment Options
Mr. Perry asked whether the projected bond interest rates were realistic. Mr. Cutter explained that the estimates came
directly from the bond bank for anticipated issuance in July. Although rates could change with market conditions, recent
estimates for comparable projects had been slightly below those used in the current schedules.

Mr. O’Connell explained that the bond bank combines financing needs from municipalities and public projects statewide to
help secure competitive rates.

Mr. Perry favored the 25-year level-debt option, noting that the approximately $50 difference in annual cost under the
longer-term option was relatively small compared with the potential $6 million in interest savings. Ms. Plansky agreed.
The School Board had not finalized its decision.

Mr. Leary opposed the project’s overall cost and scope, arguing that the work was too expensive to undertake all at once.
He cited the failed $44 million school proposal and subsequent failed $6 million police station proposal.

Mr. Gannon acknowledged affordability concerns but expressed greater confidence in the current proposal because of its
clarity, available cost information, and transparent process. He also recognized the need for school repairs and
improvements.

Project Timeline, Transparency, and Voter Education
Mr. Cutter explained that the District had initially considered presenting the project in a March 2026 warrant article but
delayed it because the School Board lacked sufficient confidence in explaining the bond schedules and full taxpayer impact.

The District subsequently chose to present financing options earlier to allow more time for voter education before the

Failed Vote Implications and Future Facility Needs

Mr. Cutter discussed alternatives if voters rejected the school funding proposal, including redistricting, changing grade

necessary work. He acknowledged the alternative of incremental repairs and stated that the District continued to maintain
Campbell through regular monitoring, responsible use of reserve funds, and timely maintenance.

Mr. Gannon discussed enrollment projections and traffic congestion, noting that even if enrollment declined, children
would continue to live in town and require educational facilities. Mr. Brunelle identified improved traffic circulation as a
potential benefit of the proposal. The Board thanked the presenters for the information and their responses to its

    E.​ Engineering Review and Escrows
    Ms. Kleiner explained that municipalities manage engineering fees either through direct billing by engineering firms
    or by collecting developer deposits into Town-held escrow accounts. The Town currently uses the latter method,
    deducting engineering fees and other applicable expenses from escrow balances. Although some municipalities prefer
    direct billing to reduce administrative workload, Town staff supported retaining the existing system. The current
    Planning and Zoning fee schedule, Appendix F, does not establish a formal escrow administration policy.

    The Town receives invoices from its engineering firm and the Nashua Regional Planning Commission (NRPC) and
    deducts charges from developers’ escrow accounts but does not routinely issue account statements. Ms. Kleiner
    recommended a formal policy addressing escrow collection, maintenance, transaction records, statements, and
    balance releases. She emphasized that interest belongs to the respective account holders and must be recorded
    accurately through coordination between the Finance Department and Planning Office. She recommended quarterly
    statements to document deposits, interest, invoices, and expenditures.

    Ms. Kleiner also recommended monitoring project progress and promptly releasing unused escrow balances. She
    proposed involving the Planning Board in developing the policy and updating the fee schedule to reflect costs
    associated with the Town’s new engineering firm. The proposed policy would go to the Planning Board for review and
    input before returning to the Board of Selectmen for final consideration.

    Mr. Perry explained that the discussion followed a question about an invoice from the Town’s former engineering firm
    for work on the Morgan Road solar array project. He supported retaining the Town’s escrow system as providing
    greater protection. Members agreed that escrow accounts were preferable to direct billing and noted that the Town
    could withhold a Certificate of Occupancy (CO) if invoices remained unpaid.

Motion:​         (Selectman J. Brunelle / Selectman D. Plansky) Moved to direct the Town Administrator and relevant
                 stakeholders to develop a formal policy and procedures for maintaining escrow accounts.
Vote: ​ ​        (5-0-0) The motion carried.

    Members differed on the Planning Board’s role in policy development. Mr. Gannon recommended that the Planning
    Board lead most of the process to minimize additional Town staff workload. Mr. Brunelle and Ms. Kleiner maintained
    that escrow administration was primarily an internal administrative function that Town staff should develop with
    Planning Board input.

    Ms. Kleiner reiterated that the policy must establish clear procedures and ensure transparency. Developers may
    request an accounting of their escrow funds, and the Town must provide the requested details. Members agreed that
    quarterly statements would improve communication and documentation while potentially reducing individual
    requests.

    Ms. Kleiner clarified that the Planning Office and Finance Department share escrow administration responsibilities.

    G.​ Donation Policy – First Read
    Ms. Kleiner explained that the existing Cash Receipts Policy governs the collection and handling of departmental
    receipts but does not address donations. The need for a separate policy arose during the 250th Anniversary
    Committee’s work and resurfaced through Spirit of Litchfield fundraising activities.

                                                                                                                        7
Litchfield Select Board​      ​       ​        ​        ​       ​        ​        ​              October 26, 2026


The Recreation Commission may solicit donations, but the Board of Selectmen must formally accept them on behalf of
the Town. Under the proposed process, fundraising groups would provide the Recreation Commission with detailed
donation records, including donor information and amounts collected. The Commission would then recommend
acceptance to the Board. Ms. Kleiner noted that the 250th Anniversary Committee had successfully followed a similar
process.

Following consultation with legal counsel, Ms. Kleiner reported that using the Recreation revolving fund for donations

supporting recreation programs did not appear to be prohibited, although the applicable statute primarily addresses
fees collected for services and programs.

The Board discussed Spirit of Litchfield’s status as a volunteer group supporting the Recreation Commission rather
than a formal subcommittee. Members noted that formal subcommittee status could trigger additional requirements
under RSA 91-A, New Hampshire’s Right-to-Know Law.

Ms. Kleiner also raised the use of Venmo to collect payments for baked goods, raffle tickets, and table reservations. The
Town had previously been advised against linking Venmo directly to a Town account. Members emphasized the need
for clear procedures to collect, document, and transfer electronic payments while maintaining appropriate financial
controls.

The Board stressed that the policy should protect the Town and volunteers through transparent accounting and
administrative procedures. Suggested safeguards included requiring two individuals to verify and sign off on collected
and counted funds. Members noted that these measures would reduce errors and promote transparency without
implying wrongdoing by volunteers.

Ms. Kleiner confirmed that Finance had reviewed the draft and that she had circulated it to Mr. Burns, Mr. Brunelle,
and Ms. Paris for feedback from those involved in fundraising. She invited further comments from staff, volunteers,
and residents.

The Board agreed to keep the Donations Policy under review and bring it back for a second reading at the next
meeting. Members acknowledged that a separate policy was necessary because donation procedures differ from those
addressed by the existing Cash Receipts Policy.

I.​ 2026 Tax Rate Review
The Board discussed the 2026 tax rate and escrow (overlay) adjustment. The Finance Director had reduced the
original $50,000 overlay to $42,237 to achieve a whole-dollar tax rate that accommodated the Town, the School
District, and the required budgets. The Town had already issued approximately $39,800 in refunds and anticipated at
least another $2,500 in abatements for properties included on the December tax bill. These additional expenses could
exceed the available overlay, raising concerns about whether the remaining balance would be sufficient.

                                                                                                                       8
    Litchfield Select Board​       ​      ​        ​        ​       ​        ​        ​              October 26, 2026


    The Board discussed how the overlay adjustment would affect the 2027 tax cap. Reducing the overlay increases the
    Town's tax effort, while increasing the overlay has the opposite effect. With an overlay of $42,237, the projected 2027
    tax cap would decrease to $47,150, compared with the current $55,500. Ms. Kleiner explained that the overlay was
    the only figure available for adjustment.

    Ms. Kleiner reported that the current municipal tax rate would be $2.71, resulting in a total tax rate of $14.34. The
    local school tax rate would remain flat at $9.73, although the state school tax rate had increased significantly. To
    increase the overlay while maintaining a properly calculated tax rate, the Town would need to raise it to $62,643.

    The Board discussed the resulting tax rate increase. Raising the overlay to $62,643 would increase the municipal tax
    rate

    by $0.01, from $2.71 to $2.72, and the total tax rate by $0.01, from $14.34 to $14.35. Board members expressed
    support for the adjustment, noting that any unused overlay would be transferred to an assigned fund. The Board
    agreed to proceed with the revised overlay and submit the updated figures, with the revised tax rate expected back by
    Wednesday morning.

    The Board discussed whether a formal motion was necessary to approve the adjustment. Members noted that
    previous overlay decisions had generally proceeded with the Board's agreement, with approval subsequently reflected
    in the signing of the tax warrants. The revised overlay would increase the projected 2027 tax cap to $73,577.
    Members agreed that increasing the overlay was preferable to exceeding it, which could require the Town to use
    revenues to cover the shortfall.

    The Board discussed the revised tax cap's effect on the upcoming budget process. Members noted that the Budget
    Committee would have approximately $73,577 available under the revised tax cap. Mr. Perry would need to explain
    the adjustment to the Budget Committee.

   D.​ Ms. Kleiner reported that the Nashua Regional Planning Commission (NRPC) was preparing a new Pavement
       Condition Index (PCI) report. She and Mr. Brown were scheduled to meet with NRPC representative Tyrell on
       November 3, 2026, and had asked him to present the report to the Board on November 9, 2026. The preliminary
       information indicated that road conditions continued to deteriorate.

   E.​ Ms. Kleiner reported that R.H. White Construction, the contractor handling gas line installation for the new Colby
       Farms project, intended to complete the work by November 15, 2026, with construction expected to begin the
       following week. The work would require crossing Albuquerque Road. The contractor had contacted Road Agent
       Brown to obtain the necessary trench permit, and the permitting arrangements had been completed.

       The Board was advised to expect significant traffic delays during construction. Members discussed questions
       raised by residents regarding the benefits of the project to Litchfield. Ms. Kleiner clarified that the gas line would
       connect to an existing line serving Continental's property rather than directly serving Litchfield. The proposed
       route would extend across Morgan Road and continue along Charles Bancroft Highway, including a crossing of
       Albuquerque Road.

       The Board discussed the potential impact on commuters who use Albuquerque Road to access Charles Bancroft
       Highway and travel toward the Everett Turnpike. Members acknowledged that construction could cause delays
       and discussed the importance of understanding the route and its effects on local traffic.

       The Board discussed ongoing construction near Charles Bancroft Highway and the planned work at the end of
       Albuquerque Avenue. Members acknowledged public complaints about construction activity but noted that the
       work appeared to be progressing appropriately and was nearing completion. The Board discussed potential
       detours and alternative routes, including Colby Road and other previously used roads, to help motorists navigate
       the affected areas.

       Mr. Brunelle suggested asking Continental to install an electronic sign to notify motorists of upcoming
       construction and potential traffic disruptions.

       The Board also discussed whether the Town could benefit from installing the natural gas line in the area. Members
       recalled that the Town might have the option to connect to the line in the future as it extends along Route 3A and
       crosses the river. However, the provider would not install a service connection without a demonstrated need. The
       Board discussed the possibility of future connections for municipal facilities, including the school project at GMS,
       as well as other commercial customers. Members noted that connecting a typical residence would require
       additional infrastructure and pressure considerations.

       Mr. Brunelle also commented favorably on the new gas station, noting its apparent popularity and steady
       customer traffic. A member reported viewing the proposed mural above the cash register and described it as
       attractive. Members also commented that the station's prices remained competitive.

   F.​ Ms. Kleiner said Bobby and Mary Jacques were being honored during a Boston Celtics game for their volunteer
       service and philanthropic contributions. The event included a public acknowledgment of the Town of Litchfield.

   G.​ Ms. Kleiner commented on the attendance at the New Hampshire Municipal Association (NHMA) conference
       scheduled for Wednesday and Thursday. She reported that, as a member of the Municipal Managers' Board, she
       would attend the annual meeting on Wednesday and return to the office on Thursday. Ms. White and Ms. Briand
       were also expected to attend on both days.

B.​ Selectman D. Plansky:
    a.​ Planning Board:
Ms. Plansky reported that the Planning Board met the previous Tuesday and continued work on the Town’s Master
Plan. The Board reviewed the economic development chapter, conservation and open space, and the Highway
Commercial District at the southern end of town.

The Planning Board voted to cancel its meeting scheduled for November 3, Election Day. Ms. Plansky reported that the
Board was fully staffed and welcomed two new members, including Ralph Boehm.

    a.​ CHEC:
Ms. Plansky noted an upcoming meeting scheduled for November 5. Members discussed a potential scheduling conflict
with the Conservation Commission and a Joint Budget and School Board meeting. The Board discussed whether to
move the meeting to November 4.

C.​ Selectman S. Perry:
     a.​ Budget Committee:
Mr. Perry also reported on the Budget Committee’s October 15 review of the Town budget. The Committee made the
following reductions:
         1.​ Planning: Notices and Advertisements, Line 555: Reduced to $450.
         2.​ General Government Buildings: Maintenance and Supplies, Line 640: Reduced by $500.
         3.​ Library: Books and Media: Reduced by $4,000.
         4.​ Fire Inspector, Line 129: Reduced by $3,482.

Mr. Perry explained that the Budget Committee reduced the Fire Inspector appropriation because the proposed budget
doubled the inspector's hours, which the Committee did not consider necessary.

Mr. Perry reported that the next Budget Committee meeting was scheduled for November 5, when the Committee
expected to begin reviewing the School District budget.

    b.​ CIP Committee:
Mr. Perry reported that the Capital Improvement Program (CIP) meeting originally scheduled for the following day had
been moved to Wednesday at 6:30 p.m. Ms. Kleiner stated that the agenda was not yet complete and that she would
distribute it the following morning.

D.​ Selectman S. Gannon:
    a.​ Conservation Commission:
Mr. Gannon reported that the Conservation Commission conducted a Route 3A roadside cleanup on Saturday, covering
the area from Pinecrest to Hillcrest. The six-person crew collected 12 bags of trash, compared with approximately 15
bags during the previous cleanup six months earlier.

The volunteers completed the work in just over one hour under favorable weather conditions.
