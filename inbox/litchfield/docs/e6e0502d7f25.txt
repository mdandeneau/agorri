6.​ Town Budget Review:
    The Committee began its Town Budget review. Members had received the budget electronically and a memorandum from
    Ms. Kleiner concerning the Board of Selectmen's work.

    4550.20 – Library
    The library presentation referenced the 2027 proposed budget spreadsheet. Library Director Anne Eule reported receiving
    $264,549 of its appropriation and providing services valued at $517,174, $1.95 of value per taxpayer dollar under the
    American Library Association calculator. Approximately 32.3% of residents have a library card.

    The library does not track individual visitors because of patron confidentiality; it tracks checkouts (physical and digital),
    activity passes, programs, reference assistance, and other facility uses. Because many visitors use the facility without
    checking out materials or presenting a card, anonymous card-at-entry collection would yield only partial data. Asked
    whether visitor counts reflect repeat visits, Ms. Eule stated that she does not see the same people every day and would
    provide available information.

    The State regulates funding: The Town raises and appropriates funds, the Board of Trustees manages them, and library
    staff operates the library. The Friends of the Library is an independent nonprofit volunteer organization, not under the
    library's authority; it may donate fundraising proceeds at its discretion.

    The library initially presented a $375,419 budget to the Board of Selectmen, driven primarily by additional insurance
    needs; the Board reduced it by $10,000, and the library made reductions in the identified areas. The current budget is a
    3.6% increase over the prior year, primarily due to insurance, with the employee contribution arrangement changed from
    80/20 to 75/25. The septic tank cleaning line was questioned because a new septic system had been completed; the
    library explained the expense was reduced as part of the $10,000 reduction, and only a small placeholder remained.

    Patron counts fell from more than 23,000 in 2024 to more than 21,000 in 2025, an approximate 7% reduction; Ms. Eule
    expected 2026 usage could rise if economic conditions weaken, while acknowledging this was based on prior experience
    rather than objective data. She reported more young families and children's program participation; Mr. Son requested a
    breakdown of children's programming and participation during the prior nine months, which the library said it tracks and
    could provide.

    The $22,000 books and media line includes Libby; Canopy, the movie service, might be accounted for separately. Ms.
    Eule agreed to provide a detailed breakdown of line 342, including licensing, service costs, and physical book purchases.
    Because of HB 1575, the Committee questioned whether new books belong in the default budget, as recurring or one-time
    expenses should be considered during the default budget discussion. Book costs are rising because several vendors have
    left the market, including one of the library's largest vendors, which closed the previous year.

    Ms. Eule reported that mornings and the after-school period are generally busy, Wednesday evenings are particularly busy,
    Tuesdays are somewhat slower, and approximately 4:00 to 6:00 p.m. is generally active. Ms. Douglas requested a
    staffing-hours breakdown by position consistent with other departments; Ms. Eule agreed.

    For the approximately 100-year-old building, the Committee stressed the need for a long-term facilities plan addressing
    major systems such as the roof and heating before emergency replacements become necessary; the library's existing
    facilities plan needs updating and expansion for long-term capital needs. The Committee recommended developing the
    plan promptly for Town review and CIP incorporation so the Town can plan for and reserve funds. The Committee also
    expressed concern that the underdeveloped facilities plan could affect the Town's ability to assess appropriate impact fees,
    noting nearly 1,000 units already involved in development activity; impact fees are based on department plans submitted
    through the facilities planning and CIP processes, with the impact fee methodology updated by the NRPC approximately
    two and a half years earlier.

    Impact Fees – Town Administrator Kim Kleiner
    Ms. Kleiner informed the Board that the Town needs to update its impact fee methodology to ensure appropriate fee
    collection; the existing study, updated in 2023 by the NRPC, focused primarily on schools and inadequately addressed the
    library, police, highway, and municipal facilities.

    The Committee reviewed follow-up questions regarding Line 342 (Programming), Line 670 (Books and Media), Children's
    Programming, and staff hours; Ms. Eule confirmed that "pages" refer to an employee role rather than an address. The
    Committee requested additional information regarding expenditures because the proposed amounts appeared unusually
    rounded, requesting a comparison of budgeted amounts, expenditures to date, and remaining balances; Ms. Eule indicated
    this information is prepared monthly for the Board of Trustees and agreed to provide it. The Committee had no further
    questions for the library and agreed that additional follow-up questions could be submitted electronically for distribution
    to the full Committee.

    4311.10 – Road Agent
    The Committee reviewed the Road Agent budget, which showed an overall increase of $158 from the prior year. Discussion
    included building maintenance and the condition of the equipment-storage building located near Sawmill Brook Park.

    Road Agent Kevin Brown reported that the building is approximately 8–10 years old and that the exterior cover has
    developed significant tears. The structure remains sound, but replacing the cover was estimated at approximately $70,000
    to $100,000. The building stores trucks, mowers, salters, salt, and other equipment. During the winter, approximately four
    large trucks are stored there.

    The Committee discussed the building's condition and whether to consider replacing it through the Capital Improvement
    Program. Mr. Brown explained that a proposed new steel or wood building, approximately 60 by 100 or 120 feet, would
    provide sufficient space to house trucks and replace the current facility. The existing building could then be retained for dry
    storage until replacement is necessary.

    4312.10 – Road Maintenance


    The Road Maintenance budget covers vehicle fuel, road maintenance and improvement, vehicle repairs, salt, and the State
    block grant; fuel increases are a cost issue, not a usage issue. The $10,000 Road Maintenance line is unchanged for many
    years and should gradually increase to supplement State funding; a $200,000 warrant article was previously not
    approved but will be requested again. The CIP includes approximately $400,000 in estimated annual road work;
    previous projects included McElwain Drive and incomplete piping work, and galvanized piping, reclamation, and drainage
    repairs remain needed.

    A recent State grant will fund an engineering inspection of underground piping, expected to begin in the spring.

    Mr. Brown said bringing road-line striping in-house saved significantly: a $35,000 quote became about $11,000 of
    completed work, including an $8,700 power unit. The Department completed major road lines, stop lines, and crosswalks
    within budget with paint to spare, approximately three times the work for a similar cost. However, in-house work
    increases demands on the three-person department.

    Of approximately ten vehicles:
        a.​ An F-450 is out of service receiving a used motor.
        b.​ A 2003 F-350 from the Fire Department needs ongoing repairs.
        c.​ A 2015 F-350 with approximately 138,000 miles has transmission and body issues.
        d.​ Aging, smaller trucks must be replaced proactively before becoming unserviceable.

    The CIP schedules replacement by age: larger vehicles after approximately ten years, smaller ones earlier, since continued
    breakdowns require costly contractor work.

    The proposed $5-per-gallon diesel price reflects current pricing; the supplier could not estimate future prices. Bulk
    purchasing beats retail rates, and off-road diesel avoids on-road fuel taxes. Police, Fire, and Highway share a supplier and
    fuel facilities, with costs tracked by department and vehicle; Highway uses on-road diesel because its vehicles may travel
    outside the State.

    The Town uses approximately 1,600 tons of salt annually at the current Morton price of $81.15 per ton, up from $77.54.
    Contracts with Morton (approximately 1,000 tons) and Easton maintain supply flexibility, with Easton covering additional
    needs during shortages.

    The State Block grant remains approximately $200,000, calculated on road miles rather than repair costs; newly
    accepted roads increase eligible mileage, and cost-based changes would require legislative action. Asphalt has risen from
    approximately $45 per ton seven years ago to more than $100 per ton, eroding the grant's purchasing power.

    4210.10 – Police Administration
    Chief Dan Jones said the full-time officer line reflects contractual increases affecting about half the fiscal year, as
    negotiations continue; increases begin July 1. The budget includes half-year funding for the School Resource Officer
    position, which was defunded the previous year; if voters approve it, the position could be filled beginning July 1, and the
    Department is pursuing a COPS grant for the position.

    Chief Dan Jones reported 6 of 14 road positions were filled when he was hired; 13 are now filled, with at least one
    additional candidate available if funding is approved.

    Overtime increased by approximately $6,000, $2,700 contractual, with the remainder primarily an additional one-half
    hour per officer per month; the Department is currently slightly overspent and has reported increased activity and
    workload. Adding an officer would not necessarily reduce overtime: the SRO cannot backfill all shifts (e.g., an evening or
    night-shift callout cannot be filled by an SRO working Monday through Friday, 7:00 a.m. to 3:00 p.m.); officers must attend
    court, complete reports, and perform other off-shift duties; staffing must cover 24-hour operations, days off, training,

    vacation, sick leave, and other requirements; major incidents such as traffic crashes generate significant overtime through
    investigations and follow-up.


    Reassigning the SRO during summer would reduce overtime only if the SRO's schedule exactly matched the shift needing
    coverage, and assignments cannot routinely change based on anticipated days off.

    Chief Jones stated he could not speak to how those projections were developed, but its staffing model differs from a
    traditional workplace: before 2022, two part-time officers worked a combined 64 hours per week (approximately 1.6
    full-time positions); one left, and the other dropped to 24 hours per week (approximately 0.6 full-time positions). The
    current request would restore approximately the previously maintained staffing level rather than increase it.

    Six positions have been added over the Chief's three years, yet overtime has continued to increase. Chief Jones reiterated
    that additional officers do not automatically eliminate overtime: approximately five officers are required to cover a
    continuous 24-hour shift, and the Department does not consistently field three officers per shift because personnel are
    assigned to Detective, SRO, Command, and other duties.

    Captain Tessier clarified that the request represents approximately $6,000, mostly for the additional one-half hour per
    officer per month, and emphasized that it seeks to restore a previously removed position, not increase staffing. The $97
    and $168 figures on the budget presentation were cumulative, with additional amounts on the following slide. The Select
    Board reduced the requested overtime allocation; the Department had requested one additional hour per officer per
    month.

    The Department has two part-time employees: a Dispatcher who assists with details and occasionally works police shifts,
    and an officer on a set 24-hour schedule; the Dispatcher is budgeted under the police support line when dispatching and
    the police officer line when on patrol. Additional part-time officers could add flexibility with fewer benefits, but qualified,
    available individuals must be found; a retired officer working only two days per week would not develop sufficient
    experience, and a part-time position could require approximately $40,000 to $50,000. The Department will provide an
    overtime breakdown by first, second, and third shifts; the day shift may run higher because specialty positions are more
    heavily staffed then.

    The Department operates six frontline cruisers:
        a.​ The oldest recently broke down at approximately 100,000 miles (engine rebuilt at approximately 47,000 to
            48,000 miles).
        b.​ Another Tahoe has approximately 40,000 to 41,000 miles.
        c.​ One pickup has approximately 24,000 miles
        d.​ The rest have fewer than 5,000 miles. (One Pickup and Two Explorers)

    A fleet and mileage breakdown will be provided. The vehicle maintenance budget was already reduced by $15,000, which
    the Department considered optimistic; replacing older vehicles could save an additional $5,000, though the account also
    covers brakes, tires, and oil changes. The vehicle in the shop should be replaced, as officers need reliable transportation; a
    new fully equipped police vehicle costs approximately $75,000 to $80,000.

    The Committee questioned the increase in Seminars and Conventions. Captain Tessier stressed that officers must complete
    24 hours of annual training under mandatory police standards, plus firearms and use-of-force instruction. Spending has
    varied year to year, and understaffing has limited training opportunities; the Department stated the budgeted amount is
    understated, with expenditures often deferred late into the fiscal year amid uncertainty about other expenses. The
    Department also considers bringing training in-house when practical.

    by moving funds to the appropriate line, and the total Dispatch coverage and Dispatch overtime remained essentially the
    same, subject to the contractual increase. He noted the hiring of a part-time Dispatcher and identified an issue with the

    Chief Jones identified significant operational and safety concerns with contracted Dispatch: Contract Dispatchers operate

    4611.20 – Conservation Commission
    Chair George Lavash requested a $2,000 increase over the prior year, supported by a 50/50 arrangement based on lease
    revenue from Town-owned conservation lands. The Town recently purchased the Durocher property and is considering
    leasing portions of it plus land adjacent to the John Bryant River Access; the lease values will be combined.

    Under the Durocher Purchase & Sales Agreement, the Conservation Commission must maintain drainage ditches to allow
    water to flow through the fields. Much maintenance can be done by hand, but some portions will require an excavator and
    additional assistance; the Commission proposed $2,000 annual increments, offset by lease revenue, to cover anticipated
    work during the year rather than setting aside funds. After touring the property, the Commission developed a staged plan
    prioritizing the most vulnerable areas; it identified about four or five ditches, with lower areas needing earlier attention.

    Asked whether the work could be done at once and replenished with lease revenue, Mr. Brennen explained it would likely
    need staging, and that lease revenue cannot simply enter the Conservation Fund; a $1,950 warrant article is
    required annually to appropriate the funds, making the current approach the most practical. Work should begin in
    November, after the foliage clears, with the first project informing the excavation scope. The Commission rejected
    requiring ditch maintenance as a lease condition: lessees might lack the equipment, and maintenance could cost more than
    lease payments.

    The Alvirne Farm Lease
    Alvirne High School leases the east-side Route 3A property containing the blueberry patch, which predated the
    Conservation Commission purchase. The farmer, Colton, performs maintenance, including mowing fields, roads, and areas
    unsuitable for corn or hay, without a separate budget allocation.

    4191.10 – Planning Board
    Ms. Kleiner mentioned that the proposed decrease of $49 is primarily for updated municipal planning books; the existing
    books are considerably outdated, and the town does not purchase updated editions every year. The overall Planning
    budget reflects a decrease, which is primarily due to the removal of seminar expenses.

    The Committee previously removed seminar expenses from the Planning and Zoning budgets and consolidated them into a
    Select Board training line; boards may request training as needed, with expenses tracked through the consolidated line
    because funds have not been used consistently from year to year.

    The Committee discussed the NRPC contract for 20 hours per month at $75 per hour; the 2027 NRPC contract was not yet
    available, although NRPC had verbally indicated it would not increase its rate. The $2,000 CIP contract expense first
    appeared in the budget in 2026, although the Town had been paying the expense before it was included in the budget.

    4191.30 – Zoning Board of Adjustment
    The Zoning budget is currently $375 lower. The seminar expense was moved to the consolidated Select Board training
    line. No other significant changes were identified.

    4195.10 – Cemeteries
    Ms. Kleiner stated that the Cemetery Trustees reported satisfaction with the existing groundskeeping contract, and the
    contractor agreed not to increase the contract price. The $1,250 monument repair allocation added the previous year
    was retained for 2027 because the funds are being used. The only increase in the Cemetery budget is for water, due to
    increased usage at the cemeteries.

    Tree removal and bench repair were questioned under the groundskeeping contract; the contract covers routine
    maintenance such as mowing and landscaping, while large-tree removal and work beyond the contractor's capacity may
    require a tree service or contractor, and the Highway Department may assist with invasive vegetation. Attachment B,
    referenced in the contract, was not included with the available documentation; staff will follow up regarding the
    attachments and related provisions. The separate maintenance and repair allocation is associated with the cemetery area
    where recently purchased burial boxes created a drainage issue. A follow-up was requested to provide the total number of
    burial boxes sold during the year.

    4197.10 – Advertising
    Ms. Kleiner commented that the $1,788 Advertising increase is driven mainly by an event held in partnership with the
    Litchfield Veterans. Last year's event succeeded with the Veterans covering the entire approximately $3,000 cost; the
    Town now proposes sharing the cost. The event connects residents with housing, rent, electricity, food, and other
    basic-needs resources, potentially reducing demand for General Assistance. Expenses include refreshments, food, signage,
    tables (one-time), and mailed letters.

    Ms. Douglas worried about the precedent of adding successful but previously unapproved events to the budget and noted
    no proven causal link between the event and General Assistance reductions. However, General Assistance was down
    approximately $12,600 year to date; state-level changes or other factors could explain it.

    This year, staff will collect attendance data and require vendors to register and identify their services to evaluate
    effectiveness. On whether the event should be annual or biennial, staff noted that other municipalities hold them annually
    as needs change; members suggested tracking vendor-table attendance without collecting names.

    4155 – Personnel
    Pension retirement rates are expected to decrease in July:
       a.​ The employee rate will decrease from 12.75% to 12.51%.
       b.​ The police rate will decrease from 30.95% to 26.71%.
       c.​ The fire rate will decrease from 29.15% to 24.63%.

    This will reduce retirement costs by $5,126.

    The budget also includes:
        a.​ Approximately a $12,678 increase in payroll taxes.
        b.​ The original proposed increase was $23,977, and after subtracting $11,299, the revised increase is $12,678.

8.​ School District Business:
    Mr. Cutter presented the Facilities Improvement Committee's plans for the Griffin Memorial School (GMS) project; the
    School Board intends to finalize the proposal for voters by the end of October or early November, in time for Budget
    Committee review and community information before the March vote.

    The 2024 warrant article for approximately $44 million failed; since then, the Committee has conducted extensive
    research, and the District contracted with Harriman and Harvey Construction for estimates, schematics, and a guaranteed
    maximum price for a renovation planned for March 2027.

    The project would remove the 19,560-square-foot first- and second-grade wing and the 8,600-square-foot portable
    classrooms; renovating the wing alone would cost about $8 million due to HVAC, roofing, and insulation issues, and the
    portables would


    be removed. The design retains and renovates the main building, adds a two-story, L-shaped addition of approximately
    equivalent square footage, creates a secondary road to improve morning traffic flow, and provides a dedicated drop-off bus
    area to reduce congestion on Route 3A. Renovations include reconfiguring the main building, relocating pre-kindergarten
    into the existing building, and using the addition for classroom space; the gymnasium and cafeteria remain structurally
    unchanged, with lighting, HVAC, and related systems in scope.

    The current projected cost is $38.9 million, including significant contingency and soft costs that may shrink as the project
    develops; the estimate will be refined in October. The previous new-building proposal was estimated at $44 million, and
    construction costs have risen substantially since. The construction cost excludes the contingency, and the estimate
    excludes interest and bonding costs.

    The $31 million facilities project budget carries multiple levels of contingency; reductions could include eliminating new
    smart boards and other nonessential improvements, and the cost is expected to drop by approximately $1 million as the
    proposal is refined. Related infrastructure, septic system, parking lot resurfacing, and site work should be completed
    during construction rather than deferred for future taxpayer funding. Construction should take about two years, with a
    possible fourth-year phase for the access road and demolition; students and programs will need to relocate as areas are
    renovated. Mr. Cutter discussed building the addition first for swing space, while some programs may relocate to other
    buildings, including continued use of the third building and its infrastructure connections.

    The School Board continues evaluating the proposal through the Facilities Committee, with bonding options up for
    discussion at the next School Board meeting; it seeks Budget Committee recommendations on bond structuring principles.
    Extending the repayment would lower the annual cost but increase the total interest, while spreading the cost across more
    benefiting residents; the objective is the lowest practical annual cost balancing principal and interest. Borrowing funds as
    needed, rather than the full amount upfront, could reduce interest paid before funds are required.

    A community forum is planned. Mr. Peeples, who serves on the School Board and the state-level Education Funding
    Committee, updated the Committee on potential reinstatement of state capital-project funding; the Board is monitoring
    possible retroactive funding and whether the shift from new construction to renovation affects eligibility. No State Building
    Aid is guaranteed.
